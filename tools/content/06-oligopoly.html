Oligopoly and Game Theory

When a few firms dominate a market, each must anticipate its rivals. Game theory gives the tools (Nash equilibrium, best responses, backward induction), and the Cournot, Bertrand and Stackelberg duopoly models are the core applications.

  • CORE The Economy: South Asia, Unit 4: §4.1–4.3 (game theory, the invisible hand game, the prisoners' dilemma), §4.6 (repeated interaction).
  • Goodwin et al. Ch. 18, §3–4 (monopolistic competition and oligopoly).

What is oligopoly?

Key ideas
  • A limited number of sellers, each with some price-making ability.
  • Difficulty of entry (economies of scale, licences, brand loyalty, network effects).
  • Strategic interdependence: with few firms, each firm's best choice depends on what the others do. This leaves room for strategic behaviour.
  • Simplest case: duopoly (two sellers). Three classic models:
    1. Cournot: firms choose quantities simultaneously.
    2. Bertrand: firms choose prices simultaneously.
    3. Stackelberg: a leader chooses quantity first, then a follower.

Indian examples: telecom (Jio, Airtel, Vi), cement, aviation (IndiGo and Air India group), cola drinks, steel.

Game theory essentials

Game theory models strategic interaction as a game: players, the alternative actions (strategies) available to each, and the payoffs each receives for every combination of actions. A simultaneous two-player game is summarised in a payoff matrix (the first number is the row player's payoff).

Definitions
  • Best response: the action that gives a player the highest payoff, given the other player's action.
  • Dominant strategy: an action that is a best response to every action of the other player.
  • Pure-strategy Nash equilibrium (lecture definition): an action profile \((a_1^*, a_2^*)\) such that player 1 cannot obtain a higher payoff by choosing an action different from \(a_1^*\), given that player 2 adheres to \(a_2^*\), and vice versa. In other words, each player's action is a best response to the other's: no one wants to deviate on their own.

Example: a pricing game (prisoners' dilemma)

Two cement firms choose a High or Low price. Payoffs are profits in ₹ crore (Firm A, Firm B):

B: High priceB: Low price
A: High price10, 102, 15
A: Low price15, 25, 5
Solving for the Nash equilibrium (best-response method)

A's best responses: if B plays High, A compares 10 (High) with 15 (Low) ⇒ Low. If B plays Low, A compares 2 with 5 ⇒ Low. Low is a dominant strategy for A (underlined).

By symmetry Low is dominant for B.

The cell where both payoffs are best responses is (Low, Low) with payoffs (5, 5): the unique Nash equilibrium.

But (High, High) gives (10, 10), better for both. The equilibrium is Pareto inefficient for the firms. This is the prisoners' dilemma (CORE §4.3): individually rational choices lead to a collectively worse outcome.

Lessons: (1) collusion (cartels) is unstable, because each member has an incentive to cheat; (2) in repeated games, threats such as "I'll match your price cut forever" (tit-for-tat, grim trigger) can sustain cooperation (CORE §4.6); (3) for consumers the firms' dilemma is good news, since competition lowers prices. Explicit cartels are illegal under India's Competition Act 2002; the Competition Commission of India has fined cement makers for cartelisation.

The Cournot model (quantity competition)

Set-up (lecture): market demand \(P = A - BQ\) with \(Q = q_a + q_b\). Both firms have the same cost function \(C(q_i) = cq_i\) (constant MC = c). Each firm chooses its quantity to maximise profit, taking the other's output as given.

Derivation: reaction functions and the Cournot–Nash equilibrium

Firm a's profit: \(\pi_a = \big[A - B(q_a + q_b)\big]q_a - cq_a\).

FOC (treating \(q_b\) as fixed): \(\dfrac{\partial\pi_a}{\partial q_a} = A - 2Bq_a - Bq_b - c = 0\). (This is MRa = MC: firm a acts as a monopolist on the residual demand left by b.)

Solve for firm a's reaction (best-response) function: $$R_a:\quad q_a = \frac{A - c - Bq_b}{2B} = \frac{A-c}{2B} - \frac{q_b}{2}.$$ By symmetry: \(R_b:\ q_b = \dfrac{A - c - Bq_a}{2B}\). Each reaction function slopes down: if your rival produces more, you produce less.

Nash equilibrium: both are on their reaction functions at once. By symmetry \(q_a = q_b = q\): \(q = \dfrac{A - c - Bq}{2B}\) ⇒ \(2Bq + Bq = A - c\) ⇒ $$\boxed{q_a^* = q_b^* = \frac{A-c}{3B}},\qquad Q^* = \frac{2(A-c)}{3B}.$$

Price: \(P^* = A - B\cdot\dfrac{2(A-c)}{3B} = \boxed{\dfrac{A + 2c}{3}}\). Profit per firm: \(\pi^* = (P^* - c)q^* = \dfrac{A-c}{3}\cdot\dfrac{A-c}{3B} = \boxed{\dfrac{(A-c)^2}{9B}}\).

The Cournot equilibrium is a Nash equilibrium: at \((q_a^*, q_b^*)\) each firm's output is a best response to the other's, so neither wants to change.

Worked example: A = 100, B = 1, c = 10
  • Reaction functions: \(q_a = 45 - q_b/2\), \(q_b = 45 - q_a/2\).
  • Solve: \(q_a = 45 - (45 - q_a/2)/2 = 22.5 + q_a/4\) ⇒ \(\tfrac34 q_a = 22.5\) ⇒ \(q_a = 30\), so \(q_b = 30\).
  • Q = 60, P = 40, each profit = (40 − 10)(30) = 900, industry profit 1800.
  • Compare monopoly: \(Q = (A-c)/2B = 45\), P = 55, profit 2025. Competition: P = c = 10, Q = 90, zero profit.

Why collusion breaks down in Cournot

Collusion as a prisoners' dilemma

If the duopolists form a cartel and split monopoly output, each produces 22.5 and earns 2025/2 = 1012.5 (> 900). But if b keeps to 22.5, a's best response is \(q_a = 45 - 22.5/2 = 33.75\): P = 100 − 56.25 = 43.75 and \(\pi_a = 33.75^2 = 1139\). Payoffs for "Collude (22.5)" vs "Cournot (30)":

b: 22.5b: 30
a: 22.51012.5, 1012.5843.75, 1125
a: 301125, 843.75900, 900

Producing 30 is dominant, so the equilibrium is (30, 30), even though (22.5, 22.5) is better for both. Cartels like OPEC face exactly this cheating problem.

Extension: Cournot with n firms

Firm i: \(\pi_i = [A - B(q_i + Q_{-i})]q_i - cq_i\). FOC: \(A - 2Bq_i - BQ_{-i} - c = 0\). In a symmetric equilibrium \(Q_{-i} = (n-1)q\): \(A - c = B(n+1)q\) ⇒ $$q = \frac{A-c}{(n+1)B},\quad Q = \frac{n}{n+1}\cdot\frac{A-c}{B},\quad P = \frac{A + nc}{n+1}.$$ For n = 1 this is monopoly and for n = 2 Cournot duopoly. As \(n \to \infty\), \(P \to c\): the competitive outcome. More firms mean less market power.

The Bertrand model (price competition)

Set-up (lecture): two firms sell an identical product with the same constant marginal cost c and choose prices simultaneously. Consumers buy from the cheaper firm. Demand facing firm 1 is

$$q_1 = \begin{cases} a - bP_1 & \text{if } P_1 < P_2 \\[2pt] 0 & \text{if } P_1 > P_2 \\[2pt] \dfrac{a - bP}{2} & \text{if } P_1 = P_2 = P \end{cases}$$

and symmetrically for firm 2.

Derivation: the Bertrand equilibrium is \(P_1 = P_2 = c\)

Check every other possibility and show that someone wants to deviate:

\(P_1 > P_2 > c\): firm 1 sells nothing. It can undercut to just below \(P_2\), capture the whole market and earn a positive profit. Not an equilibrium.

\(P_1 = P_2 = P > c\): each gets half the market. By cutting price slightly to \(P - \epsilon\), a firm gets all the market at almost the same margin, nearly doubling its profit. Not an equilibrium.

\(P_1 > P_2 = c\): firm 2 makes zero profit and could raise its price a little (still below \(P_1\)) and earn a positive profit. Not an equilibrium.

Any \(P_i < c\): that firm makes losses on every unit sold and would rather raise its price. Not an equilibrium.

\(P_1 = P_2 = c\): each earns zero. Raising price loses all customers (still zero); cutting price means losses. No profitable deviation: this is the unique Nash equilibrium.

Quantities (the "??" on the slide): total demand at P = c is \(a - bc\), shared equally, so $$\boxed{q_1 = q_2 = \frac{a - bc}{2}},\qquad \pi_1 = \pi_2 = 0.$$ With \(Q = 100 - P\) (\(a = 100\), \(b = 1\)) and c = 10: P = 10 and each firm sells 45.

The Bertrand paradox

With just two firms the outcome is the same as perfect competition: P = MC and zero profit. Real duopolies usually earn profits, so the model's assumptions are relaxed: capacity constraints (a firm cannot serve the whole market, which brings back Cournot-like results), product differentiation (prices above MC), repeated interaction (tacit collusion), and cost differences (the low-cost firm prices just below the rival's MC).

The Stackelberg model (leader and follower)

Set-up: the same demand and costs as Cournot, but firm a (the leader, e.g. a dominant incumbent) chooses its quantity first. Firm b (the follower) observes \(q_a\) and then chooses \(q_b\). The decisions are sequential, reflecting the firms' different stature. The leader produces optimally knowing the follower will make the best of the resulting situation.

Derivation: solving by backward induction

Stage 2 (follower). Given \(q_a\), b maximises \([A - B(q_a + q_b)]q_b - cq_b\), which gives its Cournot reaction function: \(q_b(q_a) = \dfrac{A - c - Bq_a}{2B}\).

Stage 1 (leader). a knows b will respond this way, so it substitutes \(q_b(q_a)\) into its own profit: $$\pi_a = \Big[A - Bq_a - B\cdot\frac{A - c - Bq_a}{2B}\Big]q_a - cq_a = \Big[\frac{A - c}{2} - \frac{Bq_a}{2}\Big]q_a.$$

FOC: \(\dfrac{A-c}{2} - Bq_a = 0\) ⇒ \(\boxed{q_a^S = \dfrac{A-c}{2B}}\): the leader produces the monopoly output.

Follower: \(q_b^S = \dfrac{A - c - (A-c)/2}{2B} = \boxed{\dfrac{A-c}{4B}}\).

Total \(Q^S = \dfrac{3(A-c)}{4B}\), price \(P^S = A - BQ^S = \boxed{\dfrac{A + 3c}{4}}\).

Profits: \(\pi_a^S = \dfrac{A-c}{4}\cdot\dfrac{A-c}{2B} = \dfrac{(A-c)^2}{8B}\) and \(\pi_b^S = \dfrac{(A-c)^2}{16B}\).

Worked example: A = 100, B = 1, c = 10

Leader \(q_a = 45\); follower \(q_b = (90 - 45)/2 = 22.5\); Q = 67.5; P = 32.5. Profits: leader (32.5 − 10)(45) = 1012.5; follower 22.5 × 22.5 = 506.25.

First-mover advantage: the leader earns 1012.5 > 900 (Cournot) by committing to a large output, which forces the follower to contract. The follower is worse off (506.25 < 900). Consumers gain: Q is higher and P lower than under Cournot.

Exam tips
  • Always write the profit function, then the FOC, then the reaction function, then solve. Show the symmetry step explicitly.
  • In Stackelberg, the leader does not take the follower's quantity as given: it substitutes the follower's reaction function. That is the whole difference from Cournot.
  • Draw the reaction-function diagram with both axes labelled, the intercepts (\((A-c)/B\) and \((A-c)/2B\)) and the equilibrium point.
  • Ranking of total output: Monopoly < Cournot < Stackelberg < Bertrand = Competition.

Summary and practice questions

Chapter summary
  • Nash equilibrium: mutual best responses. The prisoners' dilemma shows why cartels are unstable.
  • Cournot: \(q_i = (A-c)/3B\), \(P = (A+2c)/3\); with n firms \(P = (A+nc)/(n+1) \to c\).
  • Bertrand with identical products: \(P = c\), \(q_i = (a - bc)/2\), zero profit (the Bertrand paradox).
  • Stackelberg: leader \((A-c)/2B\), follower \((A-c)/4B\), \(P = (A+3c)/4\): a first-mover advantage.

Why do two Bertrand competitors end up pricing at marginal cost? Is this realistic?

At any common price above MC each firm can nearly double its profit by undercutting slightly, so only P = MC survives (show the cases). It is unrealistic when products are differentiated, capacity is limited or firms interact repeatedly, all of which let prices stay above MC.

Find all pure-strategy Nash equilibria: payoffs (row, column): (Up, Left) = (3, 3); (Up, Right) = (0, 2); (Down, Left) = (2, 0); (Down, Right) = (1, 1).

Row's best responses: vs Left → Up (3 > 2); vs Right → Down (1 > 0). Column's: vs Up → Left (3 > 2); vs Down → Right (1 > 0). Two NE: (Up, Left) and (Down, Right). This is a coordination ("assurance") game: (Up, Left) is better for both, but players may get stuck at (Down, Right).
