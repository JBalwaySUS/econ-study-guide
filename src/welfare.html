Chapter 7Welfare Economics and Market Failure

How markets order themselves, what "good" means (surplus, Pareto efficiency, the welfare theorems), and what goes wrong when the theorems' assumptions fail: market power, externalities, public goods and the commons.

Readings for this chapter
  • Goodwin et al., Principles of Economics in Context, Ch. 6 (Welfare Analysis): consumer and producer surplus, price ceilings and floors, laissez-faire and market failure.
  • CORE The Economy: South Asia, Unit 5 (§5.2 the Pareto criterion, §5.3 fairness), §8.4 (competitive equilibrium and gains from trade), §8.7 (taxes), and Unit 12 (Markets, efficiency, and public policy: §12.1–12.3 external effects, §12.5 public goods). Unit 4 §4.6 and §4.14 cover public goods games and climate change.

7.1 How markets order themselves

Class 1 opened with the campus mess-coupon market: no formal body governs buying and selling, yet coupons change hands at a fairly stable price. The mechanism is the feedback loop from Chapter 2: too high a price leaves unsold coupons, so sellers cut prices; too low a price creates a shortage, so buyers compete and prices rise. The price settles where demand equals supply, with no planner.

Adam Smith's invisible hand (assumes perfect competition)

In The Wealth of Nations (1776) Smith argued that individuals pursuing their own interest in competitive markets are "led by an invisible hand" to serve society. Bakers bake for their own gain, not out of kindness, yet we get bread. Prices signal what is scarce, and resources flow to where they are most wanted. Onion farmers want profit and buyers want a fair price; nobody plans for the city, yet Hyderabad has onions.

What it does not claim: that selfishness is always good. The outcome serves society only with competition and sound rules. The First Welfare Theorem makes this precise; Class 2 covers where it fails.

7.2 Consumer and producer surplus

  • Consumer surplus (CS): what buyers keep. It is the difference between the maximum each buyer is willing to pay (read off the demand curve) and the price actually paid, summed over all buyers. Tathya would pay up to ₹10 for onions; the price is ₹6; Tathya's surplus is ₹4.
  • Producer surplus (PS): what sellers keep. It is the price received minus the marginal cost of each unit (read off the supply curve), summed over all units. Jal can grow onions at ₹2 each and sells at ₹6, so Jal's surplus is ₹4.
  • Total surplus = CS + PS: the total gains from trade, the "size of the pie".
$$CS = \int_0^{Q^*}\big[P_d(q) - P^*\big]\,dq,\qquad PS = \int_0^{Q^*}\big[P^* - P_s(q)\big]\,dq.$$
Derivation: competitive equilibrium maximises total surplus

If quantity \(Q\) is traded, the total surplus is the area between demand (marginal benefit) and supply (marginal cost): \(TS(Q) = \int_0^{Q}\big[P_d(q) - P_s(q)\big]dq\). The price only splits this between buyers and sellers; it cancels in the total.

Maximise: \(\dfrac{dTS}{dQ} = P_d(Q) - P_s(Q) = 0\) ⇒ \(P_d(Q) = P_s(Q)\): exactly the competitive equilibrium quantity.

For \(Q < Q^*\), \(P_d > P_s\): some trades that would benefit both sides do not happen. For \(Q > Q^*\), \(P_d < P_s\): units cost more to make than they are worth to buyers.

So at equilibrium every trade where a buyer values the good more than it costs to produce actually happens (CORE §8.4). Anything that blocks trade shrinks the pie.

7.3 Taxes and deadweight loss

Derivation: the deadweight loss of a tax

With \(Q_d = a - bP_b\) and \(Q_s = c + dP_s\), a tax \(\tau\) drives a wedge \(P_b - P_s = \tau\). From Chapter 2, quantity falls by \(\Delta Q = \dfrac{bd}{b+d}\,\tau\).

The DWL is a triangle with height \(\tau\) and base \(\Delta Q\): $$DWL = \tfrac12\,\tau\,\Delta Q = \tfrac12\cdot\frac{bd}{b+d}\,\tau^2.$$

Course numbers: \(b = d = 1\), \(\tau = 2\): \(\Delta Q = 1\), DWL = 1. ✓

Two lessons: (i) DWL rises with the square of the tax, so doubling the tax quadruples the DWL; (ii) DWL is larger when demand and supply are more elastic (larger b, d). Taxing inelastic goods distorts less.

Onion vs alcohol (class question). For onions the tax is pure inefficiency: lost trades that benefited both sides. For alcohol, the "lost trades" may include consumption that harms others (drunk driving, health costs borne by the public). Then the market quantity was too high to begin with and the tax can raise welfare. That is a Pigouvian tax, covered under externalities below.

7.4 Price controls

Price ceiling (cap)

Derivation: welfare under the cap

At P = 4: \(Q_s = 4 - 2 = 2\) and \(Q_d = 10 - 4 = 6\). Trade is limited by the short side: Q = 2.

PS = \(\tfrac12(4 - 2)(2) = 2\).

CS (best case, units go to those who value them most) = area between demand and 4 for Q from 0 to 2 = \(\tfrac{(10-4) + (8-4)}{2}\times 2 = 10\).

Total = 12, so DWL = 16 − 12 = 4: the triangle between demand and supply from Q = 2 to 4, \(\tfrac12(8-4)(4-2) = 4\).

Who gets the 2 onions? With a shortage the price no longer rations, so something else does: queues, contacts, black markets, or a ration card system. If the good goes to people with lower willingness to pay, CS is even lower and the DWL larger. The class point: the cap aims to help buyers, but it can make them worse off overall.

Price floor

A price floor above equilibrium (minimum wage, minimum support prices) creates a surplus: \(Q_s > Q_d\).

India focus: minimum support prices (MSP)

Suppose the government guarantees P = 8 in our market. Then \(Q_d = 2\) and \(Q_s = 6\): a surplus of 4 units. If the government does not buy the surplus, only 2 units trade: CS = ½(10−8)(2) = 2, PS = ((8−2)+(8−4))/2 × 2 = 10, total = 12, DWL = 4. In India the Food Corporation of India procures much of the surplus wheat and rice at MSP, so farmers sell all 6 units. This supports farm incomes, but it costs the government 8 × 4 = 32 and builds large grain stocks, and it encourages over-production of the supported crops (wheat and paddy in Punjab and Haryana, with groundwater depletion: a commons problem, see the commons section). Goodwin Box 6.1 discusses the parallel debate over minimum wages.

7.5 Pareto efficiency

Definitions (CORE §5.2)
  • An allocation is Pareto efficient if no one can be made better off without making someone else worse off.
  • A Pareto improvement makes at least one person better off and nobody worse off.
  • The set of Pareto-efficient allocations forms the Pareto efficiency curve (frontier).

Coupon example: the seller values a coupon at ₹20 and the buyer at ₹40. Before trade both could gain, so the situation is not efficient. After a trade at ₹30 (seller gains 10, buyer gains 10) no free gain remains. Any price between 20 and 40 would be efficient; the price only decides the split.

Efficient is not the same as fair

Efficiency says nothing about who gets what. Choosing among points on the frontier is a question of equity, which markets do not settle; society has to (CORE §5.3). This is the same idea as a Pareto front in multi-objective optimisation.

7.6 The welfare theorems

First Fundamental Theorem of Welfare Economics

Under these assumptions, every competitive equilibrium is Pareto efficient:

  1. Perfect competition: many buyers and sellers; no one can set the price. (If this fails: market power.)
  2. No externalities: every cost and benefit falls on the people trading. (If this fails: externalities.)
  3. Full information: both sides know the price and the quality of what they trade. (If this fails: information asymmetry, e.g. used cars and "lemons", CORE §12.6.)
  4. Private goods with clear property rights: goods are rival, excludable and can be owned and traded. (If this fails: public goods and commons.)

The mess-coupon market satisfies all four: many students on WhatsApp, no spillovers, everyone knows what a coupon is. Every trade that helps both sides happens.

Second Fundamental Theorem

Under similar (plus convexity) assumptions, any Pareto-efficient allocation can be achieved as a competitive equilibrium after a suitable lump-sum redistribution of initial endowments. In short: redistribute first, then let markets work. The theorems separate efficiency (left to markets) from equity (a social choice). In practice lump-sum transfers are hard to design: real taxes create DWLs, so there is often an efficiency–equity trade-off.

Positive vs normative economics

Welfare economics is normative: "better" needs a value criterion. Even "efficient" assumes that people's own preferences are what count.

7.7 Market failure: externalities

An externality is a cost or benefit that falls on people who are not part of the transaction. Decision-makers count only their own costs and benefits, so the market makes too much of what harms others and too little of what helps them.

Negative externalityPositive externality
A factory dumps sludge in a lake; fishermen's catch falls and the factory pays nothing. Also: pollution, congestion, loud neighbours, stubble burning in Punjab affecting Delhi's air.You get vaccinated; others are protected too and you are not paid for that benefit. Also: education, a beautiful garden, R&D spillovers.
Derivation: the externality wedge

Private producers supply where price = marginal private cost (MPC). Society's cost includes the marginal external cost (MEC): \(MSC = MPC + MEC\).

Market outcome: \(MPB = MPC\). Social optimum: \(MSB = MSC\).

Course numbers: \(MPB = MSB = 10 - Q\), \(MPC = 2 + Q\), MEC = 2 ⇒ \(MSC = 4 + Q\). Market: \(10 - Q = 2 + Q\) ⇒ \(Q_m = 4\), P = 6. Optimum: \(10 - Q = 4 + Q\) ⇒ \(Q^* = 3\), P = 7.

The 4th unit costs society \(MSC(4) = 8\) but is worth only \(MSB(4) = 6\). The DWL is the triangle \(\tfrac12(8 - 6)(4 - 3) = 1\).

Derivation: the Pigouvian tax

Set a per-unit tax equal to the marginal external cost at the optimum: \(t = MEC(Q^*)\). The firm's private cost becomes \(MPC + t = MSC\), so the market now settles at \(MSB = MSC\), i.e. at \(Q^*\).

Course numbers: t = 2 shifts supply to P = 4 + Q, and the market moves to Q = 3, P = 7. Compare the ₹2 tax on onions: the same diagram, but there the tax creates a DWL, whereas here it removes one. For a positive externality, a Pigouvian subsidy equal to the marginal external benefit raises output to the social optimum (vaccination drives, scholarships, solar subsidies).

Worked example: the Coase theorem

A factory's dumping causes fishermen ₹70,000 of damage a year. A filter that stops it costs the factory ₹40,000. Efficient outcome: install the filter (40 < 70).

  • Fishermen own the right to a clean lake: the factory must either pay ₹70,000 in compensation or spend ₹40,000 on the filter. It installs the filter.
  • The factory has the right to pollute: the fishermen are willing to pay up to ₹70,000 for clean water, and the factory accepts anything above ₹40,000. They agree on a payment between 40 and 70 thousand and the filter is installed.

Coase theorem: if property rights are well defined and bargaining costs are low, private bargaining reaches the efficient outcome whatever the initial allocation of rights. The allocation of rights affects only the distribution (who pays). It fails with many affected parties (thousands of residents near a plant), high transaction costs, unclear rights or asymmetric information, and the poor may be unable to "pay" for clean air (CORE §12.2–12.3).

Derivation: cap and trade minimises abatement cost

Two firms must together cut emissions by 40 tonnes. Marginal abatement costs: \(MAC_1 = A_1\), \(MAC_2 = 3A_2\).

Total cost \(TC = \int_0^{A_1} a\,da + \int_0^{A_2} 3a\,da = \tfrac12A_1^2 + \tfrac32A_2^2\), subject to \(A_1 + A_2 = 40\).

Minimising: \(\dfrac{d}{dA_1}\big[\tfrac12A_1^2 + \tfrac32(40 - A_1)^2\big] = A_1 - 3(40 - A_1) = 0\) ⇒ \(\boxed{MAC_1 = MAC_2}\) ⇒ \(A_1 = 30\), \(A_2 = 10\).

Cost = ½(900) + 1.5(100) = 450 + 150 = 600. A uniform rule (20 each) costs ½(400) + 1.5(400) = 200 + 600 = 800.

With tradable permits, each firm abates until its MAC equals the permit price. The market price settles at 30 where the cap is met. The low-cost firm does more of the cutting and sells permits to the high-cost firm. A carbon tax of 30 per tonne would give the same allocation.

Public goods and free-riding

Derivation: the efficient quantity of a public good (Samuelson condition)

Because the good is non-rival, every unit benefits all users at once. The social marginal benefit is the vertical sum of individual marginal benefits: \(MSB(Q) = \sum_i MB_i(Q)\). (For private goods we sum demand curves horizontally.)

Efficiency: \(\boxed{\sum_i MB_i = MC}\).

Example: three residents value street lights at \(MB_1 = 60 - Q\), \(MB_2 = 40 - Q\), \(MB_3 = 20 - Q\); MC = 75 per light. \(\sum MB = 120 - 3Q = 75\) ⇒ \(Q^* = 15\).

Private provision: even resident 1, who values lights most, would buy only where \(60 - Q = 75\), i.e. none. And because the good is non-excludable, each resident hopes the others will pay and free-rides. Result: under-provision, often zero.

Solutions: tax-financed public provision; community norms with peer punishment of free-riders (CORE §4.7 shows experimentally that punishment sustains contributions); making the good excludable (turning it into a club good).

The tragedy of the commons

A shared lake with one fish stock: each user keeps the whole gain from fishing more but shares the cost (fewer fish for everyone), so each over-uses and everyone ends worse off. It is a negative externality with no owner to charge for it, and a many-player prisoners' dilemma.

Derivation: open access vs the efficient number of boats

With \(N\) boats, total catch value is \(N\cdot AP(N)\) with \(AP = 100 - N\) (₹ thousand per boat, falling because the boats share a fixed stock). Each boat costs 20.

Open access: a new boat enters as long as its own catch covers its cost: \(AP(N) \ge 20\) ⇒ entry until \(100 - N = 20\) ⇒ \(N_{open} = 80\). Total rent = \(80(20 - 20) = 0\): the resource's value is completely dissipated.

Efficient: maximise total rent \(R = N(100 - N) - 20N\). \(dR/dN = 100 - 2N - 20 = 0\) ⇒ \(N^* = 40\). Equivalently set the marginal product \(MP = 100 - 2N\) equal to cost. Rent = \(40(60 - 20) = 1600\).

Why the gap: the entrant compares its average catch with cost and ignores that it lowers every other boat's catch by \(|dAP/dN| = 1\) each. That is an external cost of \(N\) per entrant. Open access gives twice the efficient effort here.

Elinor Ostrom (Nobel 2009) documented many successful community-managed commons, such as irrigation systems in Nepal and forest panchayats in India's hills. She showed that the "tragedy" is not inevitable when users can communicate, monitor and sanction one another.

Why climate change is harder

  • No world government: no one can enforce rules across countries, so agreements rely on voluntary compliance.
  • Free-riding by countries: every country gains from others' cuts, so each is tempted to wait (CORE §4.14).
  • Costs now, benefits later: cuts pay off decades away, mostly for people not yet born.
  • Unequal responsibility: countries differ in who caused the problem, who is hurt most and who can afford to act. This is India's "common but differentiated responsibilities" position.
  • Uncertainty and tipping points: damage is hard to measure, and some changes may be irreversible.

7.9 Summary and practice questions

Chapter summary
  • CS = area under demand above price; PS = area above supply below price. A competitive equilibrium maximises CS + PS.
  • Tax DWL = ½τΔQ = ½τ²·bd/(b+d). Price ceilings and floors create shortages or surpluses and DWL, plus rationing problems.
  • Pareto efficiency: no free improvements. Efficient ≠ fair.
  • First Welfare Theorem: competition + no externalities + full information + private goods ⇒ Pareto efficiency. Second: any efficient allocation is reachable via redistribution plus markets.
  • Externalities: market Q where MPB = MPC; optimum where MSB = MSC. Fixes: Pigouvian tax t = MEC(Q*), Coase bargaining, cap and trade (equalises MACs).
  • Public goods: \(\sum MB_i = MC\), so free-riding leads to under-provision. Commons: open access leads to over-use (AP = cost instead of MP = cost).
Demand P = 20 − Q, supply P = 4 + Q. Compute the equilibrium, CS, PS, and the effects of a ₹4 per-unit tax (prices, quantity, revenue, DWL).

Equilibrium: 20 − Q = 4 + Q ⇒ Q = 8, P = 12. CS = ½(8)(8) = 32, PS = 32, TS = 64. Tax 4: 20 − Q = 8 + Q ⇒ Q = 6, buyers pay 14, sellers get 10. CS = ½(6)(6) = 18, PS = 18, revenue = 24, total 60, DWL = 4 (= ½ × 4 × 2 ✓).

In the same market a price ceiling of ₹8 is imposed. Find the shortage and the DWL (assume efficient rationing).

Qs = 8 − 4 = 4, Qd = 20 − 8 = 12: shortage 8. Trade 4. PS = ½(8 − 4)(4) = 8. CS = area between demand and 8 over 0–4 = ((12 + 8)/2)(4) = 40. Total 48 vs 64: DWL = 16 = ½(16 − 8)(8 − 4) ✓.

State the First Welfare Theorem and give one real example for the failure of each assumption.

Competitive equilibria are Pareto efficient if there is (1) perfect competition (failure: a pharmaceutical patent monopoly), (2) no externalities (failure: stubble burning and Delhi's air), (3) full information (failure: used-car "lemons", health insurance), (4) private goods with clear property rights (failure: groundwater over-extraction, street lighting).

Explain why the Coase theorem says the allocation of property rights does not matter for efficiency. Why might it still matter in practice?

With costless bargaining the parties will always strike the deal that maximises joint surplus, and the rights only decide who pays whom. In practice transaction costs, many affected parties, unclear rights, information problems and wealth constraints (the poor cannot pay polluters) prevent efficient bargains, and the distribution of rights is itself a fairness question.

Exit question from Class 1: give an example where a market outcome is efficient but you would call it unfair.

Sample answer: during a flood, bottled water sells at a very high competitive price; every trade is voluntary and the allocation may be efficient, but poorer families go without. Or: perfect price discrimination is efficient but leaves consumers no surplus. Efficiency concerns the size of the pie, fairness its division.
