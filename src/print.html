Chapter 1Inflation, the Phillips Curve and Monetary Policy

What inflation is, why it matters, what causes it (too much demand or a fight over who gets what), how it is linked to unemployment, and how central banks such as the RBI use the policy interest rate to control it.

Readings for this chapter
  • Mitchell, Wray & Watts, Macroeconomics, Ch. 17 (Unemployment and Inflation) and Ch. 18 (The Phillips Curve and Beyond).
  • CORE The Economy: South Asia, Unit 15: §15.1–15.7 (inflation, conflicting claims, Phillips curve, supply shocks) and §15.8–15.11 (monetary policy, exchange-rate channel, demand shocks, inflation targeting). Unit 14 §14.1–14.5 for the multiplier model.

1.1 Key concepts and measurement

Key ideas: definitions
  • Inflation: a persistent (continuing) increase in the general price level. A one-off price rise is not inflation (Mitchell et al. §17.2).
  • Zero inflation: the price level is constant from year to year.
  • Deflation: a fall in the general price level (inflation is negative).
  • Disinflation: a fall in the rate of inflation. Prices still rise, only more slowly (e.g. 8% → 5%).
  • Hyperinflation: extreme, accelerating inflation (the course uses >40–50%; per month in some definitions). Examples: Weimar Germany in the 1920s, Zimbabwe in the 2000s.
  • Real interest rate = nominal interest rate − inflation rate (the Fisher equation).

Measuring inflation

The price level is measured by a price index, usually the Consumer Price Index (CPI): the cost of a fixed basket of goods in year \(t\) relative to its cost in a base year, times 100. The inflation rate between two years is the percentage change in the index:

$$\pi_t = \frac{P_t - P_{t-1}}{P_{t-1}} \times 100\%.$$

If the CPI rises from 150 to 159, inflation is \((159-150)/150 = 6\%\). Mitchell et al. also distinguish a stable inflation rate (prices rise 10% every month), an accelerating rate (10%, 11%, 12%…) and a decelerating rate (10%, 9%, 8%…, which is disinflation).

The Fisher equation

Derivation: real and nominal interest rates

Lend ₹1 today at nominal rate \(i\). Next year you receive \(₹(1+i)\).

Prices rise by \(\pi\), so ₹1 of goods today costs \(₹(1+\pi)\) next year. The goods your repayment can buy are \(\dfrac{1+i}{1+\pi}\) units of today's goods.

The real return \(r\) is defined by \(1 + r = \dfrac{1+i}{1+\pi}\), i.e. \((1+r)(1+\pi) = 1+i\).

Expanding: \(1 + r + \pi + r\pi = 1 + i\). When \(r\) and \(\pi\) are small, \(r\pi\) is negligible (e.g. \(0.04 \times 0.06 = 0.0024\)), so $$\boxed{\,r \approx i - \pi\,}$$

Decisions are made before inflation is known, so they use expected inflation: \(r = i - \pi^e\) (the ex-ante real rate; CORE §15.8).

Worked example (CORE §15.1)

A loan carries a nominal rate of 10% and inflation is 6%. Real rate \(\approx 10 - 6 = 4\%\). Exact: \(1.10/1.06 - 1 = 3.77\%\). If inflation rises to 12%, the real rate is \(-2\%\): the lender is repaid in rupees that buy less than the rupees lent.

1.2 What's wrong with inflation, and with deflation?

Costs of inflation

  • Fixed nominal incomes lose value. A pensioner on ₹20,000 a month has real income \(20{,}000/P\). With 10% inflation, after one year it buys only \(20{,}000/1.1 \approx ₹18{,}182\) of last year's goods.
  • Redistribution between debtors and creditors. Unexpected inflation lowers the real value of a fixed nominal debt, \(D/P\). This helps borrowers and hurts lenders (see the box below).
  • Uncertainty. High inflation is usually also volatile, which makes long-term contracts and investment planning risky.
  • Confusing price signals. When all prices rise quickly, producers find it hard to tell whether a rise in their price is a change in relative price (a signal to produce more) or just general inflation, so resources are misallocated.
  • Menu costs. Firms must reprint price lists, relabel and renegotiate more often. Related shoe-leather costs come from people holding less cash and making more trips to the bank.
Derivation: who gains from unexpected inflation?

A borrower agrees a nominal rate \(i\) expecting inflation \(\pi^e\), so the expected real rate is \(r^e = i - \pi^e\). The realised real rate is \(r = i - \pi\). The difference is $$r - r^e = -(\pi - \pi^e).$$ If inflation turns out higher than expected (\(\pi > \pi^e\)), the realised real rate is lower than agreed. The lender's real wealth is transferred to the borrower. Example: \(i = 8\%\), \(\pi^e = 2\%\) (expected real rate 6%), actual \(\pi = 6\%\): realised real rate 2%. On a ₹1,00,000 loan, about ₹4,000 of real value moves from lender to borrower in one year.

Costs of deflation

Deflation can be even more damaging than moderate inflation:

  1. Postponed spending. If prices are expected to fall, households delay buying (especially durables like cars and appliances) because they will be cheaper later. This acts like a negative aggregate demand shock, lowering output, which can push prices down further.
  2. Rising real debt burden. The real value of debt \(D/P\) rises when \(P\) falls. Indebted households cut consumption to get back to their target wealth (CORE Unit 14), reinforcing the fall in demand.
  3. Monetary policy gets stuck. Since \(r = i - \pi\), with \(\pi < 0\) the real rate is above the nominal rate. Nominal rates cannot go much below zero (the zero lower bound, CORE §15.8), so the central bank cannot bring the real rate down enough to revive spending. Japan's long deflation from the 1990s is the classic case.

1.3 Demand-pull inflation and the quantity theory of money

Demand-side (demand-pull) explanations assume the economy is already at full capacity, including full employment. Then extra spending cannot raise output, so it raises prices. Sources of excess demand listed in the lecture:

  • a large increase in government expenditure;
  • an abrupt change in behaviour that raises consumption and reduces saving;
  • easier credit from the banking system to the government or households, typically due to monetary expansion by the central bank.

Keynes (How to Pay for the War, 1940) called the excess of planned spending over the output available at full capacity the inflationary gap.

The equation of exchange

$$M V = P Y$$

\(M\) = money stock, \(V\) = velocity (the average number of times each rupee is spent on final output in a year), \(P\) = price level, \(Y\) = real output. \(PY\) is nominal GDP. As written this is an identity: velocity is defined as \(V = PY/M\), so it is always true and explains nothing on its own (Mitchell et al. §17.4).

Velocity example (Mitchell et al. Box 17.1)

An economy has ₹100 of money and two people. A buys ₹100 of goods from B, then B buys ₹100 of goods from A. Total spending is ₹200 with only ₹100 of money, so each rupee was used twice: \(V = 200/100 = 2\).

Derivation: from identity to the quantity theory, \(\pi = \mu - g\)

Take natural logs: \(\ln M + \ln V = \ln P + \ln Y\).

Differentiate with respect to time. The growth rate of a variable is \(d\ln X/dt = \dot X/X\), written \(\hat X\): $$\hat M + \hat V = \hat P + \hat Y.$$ (So: money growth + velocity growth = inflation + real growth.)

Quantity theory assumptions (classical). (a) \(V\) is constant, set by payment habits, so \(\hat V = 0\). (b) \(Y\) is fixed at full employment by real forces (labour market and technology), so \(\hat Y = g\) is independent of money. This separation of real from nominal is the classical dichotomy.

Then \(\hat P = \pi\) and $$\boxed{\,\pi = \hat M - g = \mu - g\,}$$ Inflation equals money growth minus real output growth. With \(Y\) also constant, doubling \(M\) doubles \(P\). Causation runs from money to prices: "inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon" (Friedman, Monetarism).

Example: money grows 12% a year and real GDP 7% a year, with stable velocity: predicted inflation = 12 − 7 = 5%.

Critiques you should be able to state (Mitchell et al.)
  1. Velocity is not constant. US M2 velocity swung between about 1.5 and 2.2 between 1960 and 2015, so there is no stable proportional link between \(M\) and \(P\).
  2. Economies are rarely at full employment. With spare capacity, firms meet extra spending by producing more (\(Y\) rises), not by raising prices.
  3. Causation may run the other way. Banks create deposits when they lend, so money responds to demand for credit (endogenous money): rising wages and prices lead firms to borrow more, raising \(M\).

1.4 Cost-push inflation and inflation as social conflict

Supply-side (cost-push) explanations: inflation starts when costs rise, for example

  • higher prices of raw materials (oil) or of wage goods such as food, which push up wage demands;
  • an autonomous push by firms to raise their profit margin (mark-up), or by unionised workers to raise wages.

The process then evolves as a wage–price spiral (workers push first) or a price–wage spiral (firms push first). Its course depends on the relative bargaining power of firms (capitalists) and workers. On this view inflation is essentially social conflict over the shares of the value produced (Kalecki, Lerner, Rowthorn; Mitchell et al. §17.3; CORE §15.2).

Mark-up pricing

Derivation: prices, wages and productivity

Let \(W\) be the money wage, \(\lambda = Y/N\) labour productivity (output per worker). Unit labour cost is \(W/\lambda\).

Firms with price-setting power add a mark-up \(\mu\) to unit cost: \(P = (1+\mu)\dfrac{W}{\lambda}\).

Take logs and growth rates: \(\hat P = \widehat{(1+\mu)} + \hat W - \hat\lambda\). With a constant mark-up, $$\boxed{\,\pi = \hat W - \hat\lambda\,}$$ Wages can grow as fast as productivity without causing inflation. This is the basis of incomes policies that tie wage growth to productivity growth.

The wage share in output is \(\omega = \dfrac{WN}{PY} = \dfrac{W}{P\lambda} = \dfrac{1}{1+\mu}\). A higher mark-up means a lower wage share. The mark-up is the distribution of income between profits and wages.

A simple conflicting-claims model

Derivation: incompatible claims generate steady inflation

Workers want a wage share \(\omega_w\). Firms, through their target mark-up, want to leave workers only \(\omega_f = 1/(1+\mu_f)\). Claims are incompatible when \(\omega_w > \omega_f\): together they claim more than 100% of output.

Wage setting. Workers push money wages up faster the further the actual share \(\omega\) is below their target, with bargaining strength \(\theta_w > 0\): \(\hat W = \theta_w(\omega_w - \omega)\).

Price setting. Firms raise prices faster the further the actual share exceeds what their mark-up allows, with strength \(\theta_f > 0\): \(\hat P = \theta_f(\omega - \omega_f)\).

The wage share changes as \(\hat\omega = \hat W - \hat P - \hat\lambda\). Assume \(\hat\lambda = 0\) for simplicity. In a steady state the share is constant, \(\hat\omega = 0\), so \(\hat W = \hat P\): $$\theta_w(\omega_w - \omega^*) = \theta_f(\omega^* - \omega_f).$$

Solve for the distribution: $$\omega^* = \frac{\theta_w\,\omega_w + \theta_f\,\omega_f}{\theta_w + \theta_f},$$ a weighted average of the two targets, weighted by bargaining power.

Substitute back to get inflation: $$\boxed{\,\pi^* = \theta_f(\omega^* - \omega_f) = \frac{\theta_w\,\theta_f}{\theta_w + \theta_f}\,(\omega_w - \omega_f)\,}$$

Interpretation. (i) Inflation is positive only if claims are incompatible (\(\omega_w > \omega_f\)); its size is proportional to this aspiration gap. (ii) Neither side gets its target. The actual share lies between them, closer to the stronger side. (iii) Stronger unions (higher \(\theta_w\)) raise both the wage share and inflation. (iv) Anything that lowers bargaining power, such as unemployment, reduces inflation. That is the link to the Phillips curve.

Example: \(\omega_w = 0.65\), \(\omega_f = 0.60\), \(\theta_w = \theta_f = 1\). Then \(\omega^* = 0.625\) and \(\pi^* = \tfrac12(0.05) = 2.5\%\) per period. If unions strengthen to \(\theta_w = 3\): \(\omega^* = (1.95 + 0.6)/4 = 0.6375\), \(\pi^* = \tfrac34(0.05) = 3.75\%\).

The CORE version: wage-setting, price-setting and the bargaining gap

CORE (§15.2–15.3, building on Unit 9) puts the same idea in a labour-market diagram:

  • The wage-setting curve (WS) gives the real wage employers must pay to get workers to work hard. It rises with employment: with fewer unemployed, losing your job costs less, so a higher wage is needed.
  • The price-setting curve (PS) gives the real wage that remains once firms set their profit-maximising mark-up. It is flat (it depends on productivity and the degree of competition).
  • Labour-market equilibrium is where WS = PS. The corresponding unemployment rate is the inflation-stabilising rate \(u^*\).
Key result: the bargaining gap $$\text{bargaining gap (\%)} = \frac{w_{WS} - w_{PS}}{w_{PS}}\times 100$$

Example (CORE §15.3): WS wage 101, PS wage 100 ⇒ gap 1%. Money wages rise 1% and prices then rise 1% to restore the mark-up, so inflation is 1% a period for as long as the gap persists. With expectations built in (CORE §15.6):

$$\boxed{\,\text{inflation} = \text{expected inflation} + \text{bargaining gap}\,}$$

Below \(u^*\) the gap is positive and inflation rises; at \(u^*\) the gap is zero and inflation is constant; above \(u^*\) the gap is negative and inflation falls.

Supply shocks

A rise in the world price of an imported input (oil) is a loss of real income to the nation: there is less output left to share between domestic wages and profits (CORE §15.7; Mitchell et al. §17.3). In WS–PS terms, the PS curve shifts down. At the old unemployment rate a positive bargaining gap opens, so inflation rises and, if policy tightens, unemployment rises. This is stagflation, as after the 1973 OPEC oil shock (US inflation rose from 6.2% to 9.1% and unemployment from 4.9% to 8.5% between 1973 and 1975). The question is who bears the loss. If each side tries to pass it on to the other, a spiral follows.

Policy responses to a wage–price spiral

  • Accommodate: keep interest rates and fiscal policy unchanged. Banks keep lending, money growth follows the spiral, and inflation persists. Note that money is the fuel here, not the spark.
  • Discipline with unemployment (the NAIRU strategy): tighten monetary and fiscal policy. Higher unemployment weakens workers' bargaining power and the spiral is "choked off", at the cost of lost output and jobs.
  • Incomes policies: negotiated or imposed guidelines tying wage and price growth to productivity (US wage–price guideposts in 1962, the Nixon freezes in 1971–74, the Scandinavian model of centralised bargaining). These worked best where tripartite bargaining institutions were strong.

1.5 The Phillips curve

Bill Phillips (1958) plotted British data for 1861–1957 and found an inverse, non-linear relationship between unemployment and the rate of change of money wages. Later economists restated it as a relationship between price inflation and unemployment.

Intuition: as the economy expands and unemployment falls, demand for labour outstrips supply. Wages are bid up and passed into prices through a wage–price spiral. Inflationary pressure actually started once unemployment fell below a positive threshold, not zero. This threshold later became known as the natural rate of unemployment.

Derivation: Phillips curve algebra (Mitchell et al. §18.2)

Original wage Phillips curve (non-linear): \(\hat W_t = \alpha_0 + \beta\,u_t^{-1}\), with \(\beta > 0\) and \(\alpha_0 < 0\). As \(u \to \infty\), wage inflation approaches \(\alpha_0\).

Use the mark-up result \(\pi = \hat W - \hat\lambda\). Write productivity growth as \(\gamma\): $$\pi_t = \alpha_0 - \gamma + \beta u_t^{-1} = \alpha + \beta u_t^{-1},\qquad \alpha = \alpha_0 - \gamma.$$

Linear version: \(\hat W_t = \alpha_0 - \beta u_t\), so the price Phillips curve is $$\pi_t = \alpha - \beta u_t,\qquad \alpha = \alpha_0 - \gamma > 0.$$ Inflation is higher when unemployment is lower and when productivity growth is lower.

Instability and stagflation

US data fit a stable downward-sloping curve for 1948–69. But in 1970–80 high inflation and high unemployment occurred together. This stagflation violated the supposed trade-off, and after 1981 no stable relationship was visible at all. Explanations: (1) the curve shifted because inflation expectations rose (Friedman, next section); (2) supply shocks (OPEC oil prices quadrupled in 1973) shifted the PS curve; (3) conflict over income shares intensified as productivity growth slowed (Mitchell et al. §17.3). Lerner had already noted in 1951 that high inflation and high unemployment could co-exist.

1.6 Expectations and the Phillips curve

Milton Friedman (1968) and Edmund Phelps argued from a monetarist standpoint that the inflation–unemployment trade-off is only short-run. The key assumptions:

  • Workers care about the real wage \(W/P\), not the money wage.
  • In an inflationary episode, money wages rise less than prices, so the real wage falls and firms (who know prices) hire more.
  • Through imperfect information (money illusion), workers initially see only their higher money wage and supply the same or more labour.
  • Soon workers realise that their real wage has fallen, revise their expected inflation upwards and cut labour supply, so unemployment returns to its natural rate.

The mechanism, stage by stage

  1. Stage 1 (point A). Actual inflation = expected inflation (2%); unemployment is at the natural rate \(u^*\).
  2. Stage 2 (A → B). The government tries to exploit the trade-off with expansionary policy to cut unemployment to \(u_1\). Inflation rises to 5%. Workers see higher money wages and work more; firms see a lower real wage and hire more.
  3. Stage 3 (B → C). Workers learn that actual inflation is 5%, revise \(\pi^e\) up to 5% and demand higher money wages. The real wage rises back and employment falls: unemployment returns to \(u^*\) but inflation stays at 5%. The economy is now on a new, higher SRPC.
  4. Repeating the attempt gives C → D → E. In the long run the trade-off disappears: the LRPC is vertical at \(u^*\). Holding \(u < u^*\) requires ever-accelerating inflation; hence the accelerationist hypothesis.
Derivation: expectations-augmented Phillips curve and the natural rate

Add expected inflation to wage bargaining (Mitchell et al. eq. 18.5): \(\hat W_t = \alpha_0 - \beta u_t + \phi\,\pi^e_t\), \(0 \le \phi \le 1\). Here \(\phi\) is the share of expected inflation that workers build into wage claims.

Price inflation (subtract productivity growth): the EAPC $$\pi_t = \alpha - \beta u_t + \phi\,\pi^e_t.$$ A rise in \(\pi^e\) shifts the short-run curve up by \(\phi\,\Delta\pi^e\).

Long run means expectations are correct: \(\pi^e_t = \pi_t = \pi\). Substituting: \(\pi(1-\phi) = \alpha - \beta u\), so $$\pi = \frac{\alpha}{1-\phi} - \frac{\beta}{1-\phi}\,u.$$ The long-run slope \(-\beta/(1-\phi)\) is steeper than the short-run slope \(-\beta\).

If \(0 < \phi < 1\) (the Keynesian position) a steeper long-run trade-off still exists. If \(\phi = 1\) (the monetarist position), \(\pi(1-1) = 0 = \alpha - \beta u\), so the only possible long-run unemployment rate is $$\boxed{\,u^* = \frac{\alpha}{\beta}\,}$$ The LRPC is vertical. \(u^*\) depends only on structural factors: productivity growth (through \(\alpha\)) and wage responsiveness \(\beta\). Higher productivity growth lowers \(\alpha\) and so lowers \(u^*\).

With \(\phi = 1\) we can rewrite the EAPC around \(u^*\) (since \(\alpha = \beta u^*\)): $$\pi_t - \pi^e_t = -\beta\,(u_t - u^*).$$ Inflation exceeds expectations only when unemployment is below the natural rate.

How are expectations formed?

Adaptive expectations (Friedman): \(\pi^e_{t+1} = \pi^e_t + \lambda(\pi_t - \pi^e_t)\), \(0 < \lambda \le 1\). People correct a fraction \(\lambda\) of last period's forecast error. With \(\lambda = 1\), \(\pi^e_t = \pi_{t-1}\) (expect last year's inflation), and the EAPC becomes $$\pi_t - \pi_{t-1} = -\beta(u_t - u^*) \quad\Rightarrow\quad \Delta\pi_t = -\beta(u_t - u^*).$$

This is the accelerationist Phillips curve: it is the change in inflation, not its level, that depends on unemployment. That is why \(u^*\) is also called the NAIRU (non-accelerating-inflation rate of unemployment); CORE calls it the inflation-stabilising rate of unemployment.

\(u^* = 5\%\), \(\beta = 1\), inflation starts at 2%. The government holds unemployment at 3% from year 1, so \(\Delta\pi = -1\times(3-5) = +2\) points every year.

Rational expectations (New Classical economics, 1970s): people use all available information, including knowledge of the model and of policy, so forecast errors are purely random. A predictable policy cannot fool anyone even in the short run: the economy stays on the vertical LRPC (policy ineffectiveness).

Critiques (Mitchell et al. §18.3–18.4)
  • Quit rates are pro-cyclical. Friedman's story implies workers quit more in recessions, when they wrongly think real-wage offers are too low. In fact quits rise in booms (Thurow).
  • Hysteresis. Estimates of the NAIRU tend to follow actual unemployment, e.g. jumping after the 1974 recession. Long spells of unemployment erode skills and attachment to work, so the "natural" rate itself depends on demand history and is not independent of policy.
  • Rational expectations demand implausible knowledge and ignore genuine uncertainty.

1.7 The scope of monetary policy

  • Original idea: the central bank (CB) changes the money stock through open market operations: buying government bonds injects money and selling them withdraws it.
  • From the 1980s: most CBs instead set a short-term policy interest rate (in India the repo rate, at which the RBI lends to banks overnight) and supply whatever reserves are needed at that rate.
  • Uses: to boost demand and counter recessions, and, in contemporary practice, mainly to control inflation.
  • Inflation targeting: many CBs (from New Zealand in 1990, then the UK, Canada and others; 28 countries by CORE's count) have an explicit inflation target, often 2% in rich countries. Some give inflation an exclusive mandate, largely giving up the goal of boosting output and employment. The period from the 1980s to 2008 of low, stable inflation is called the Great Moderation (CORE §15.11).

  2. Asset prices: higher rates lower the present value of future income, so bond, share and house prices fall. Households feel poorer and consume less (wealth effect), and collateral shrinks.
  3. Expectations and confidence: a credible CB that tightens signals that inflation will be controlled, which moderates wage and price setting.
  4. Exchange rate (CORE §15.9): higher domestic rates attract foreign capital and the currency appreciates. Exports become dearer and imports cheaper: net exports fall (less demand) and import prices fall, lowering inflation directly.

Monetary policy in the multiplier model

The slide's model is CORE's open-economy multiplier model (Unit 14 §14.5). Aggregate demand is

$$AD = c_0 + c_1(1-t)Y + I(r) + G + X - mY,$$

with autonomous consumption \(c_0\), marginal propensity to consume \(c_1\), tax rate \(t\), investment depending negatively on the real interest rate \(I(r)\), government spending \(G\), exports \(X\) and marginal propensity to import \(m\).

Derivation: equilibrium output and the multiplier

Goods-market equilibrium: output equals planned spending, \(Y = AD\) (the 45° line).

\(Y = c_0 + c_1(1-t)Y + I(r) + G + X - mY\). Collect the \(Y\) terms: $$Y\big[1 - c_1(1-t) + m\big] = c_0 + I(r) + G + X.$$

Solve: $$Y^* = \underbrace{\frac{1}{1 - c_1(1-t) + m}}_{k\ \text{(multiplier)}}\;\big[c_0 + I(r) + G + X\big].$$

Any change in autonomous spending changes output by \(k\) times as much: \(\Delta Y = k\,\Delta c_0\), \(k\,\Delta G\), or \(k\,\Delta I\). Taxes and imports are leakages that make \(k\) smaller. With no government or trade (\(t = m = 0\)), \(k = 1/(1-c_1)\).

Monetary policy: a cut in the policy rate lowers \(r\) and raises investment: \(\Delta Y = k\,[I(r') - I(r)]\). With a linear investment function \(I = \bar I - a r\): \(\Delta Y = -k\,a\,\Delta r\).

Worked example (CORE §14.2, §14.5)

Basic model: \(c_1 = 0.6\) gives \(k = 1/(1-0.6) = 2.5\), which CORE calls unrealistically large. Adding \(t = 0.2\) and \(m = 0.1\): \(1 - 0.6(0.8) + 0.1 = 0.62\), so \(k = 1/0.62 \approx 1.6\).

Recession and response. Households' confidence falls and \(c_0\) drops by 10 (₹ thousand crore): \(\Delta Y = -1.6 \times 10 = -16\). If investment rises by 5 for every 1-point cut in \(r\) (\(a = 5\)), the RBI needs \(\Delta r = -\Delta Y_{\text{target}}/(k\,a) = -16/(1.6\times5) = -2\) points to fully offset the shock.

Limits: (i) the zero lower bound: if \(i\) is already near 0, the CB cannot cut further, so it may turn to quantitative easing (buying bonds to lower long-term rates); (ii) when firms and households are pessimistic, investment may respond weakly to lower rates (Keynes's "pushing on a string"); (iii) countries in a currency union (the Eurozone) have no national monetary policy (CORE §15.8).

1.8 India: the RBI and flexible inflation targeting

India focus
  • In May 2016 the RBI Act, 1934 was amended to give flexible inflation targeting (FIT) a statutory basis. The amendment came into force on 27 June 2016, and the target was first notified in August 2016.
  • Target: 4% CPI (headline) inflation, with a tolerance band of ±2% (2–6%). Under Section 45ZA the Central Government sets the target in consultation with the RBI, for five years at a time. It was renewed for 2021–26, and news reports say it has been renewed for 2026–31.
  • The policy rate (the repo rate) is set by a six-member Monetary Policy Committee: three RBI members and three external members.
  • "Flexible" means the RBI also weighs growth: it aims to bring inflation back to target over time rather than at any cost, especially after supply shocks such as food-price spikes.
  • Outcomes reported in the RBI's own 2025 review paper: CPI inflation averaged about 4.9% under FIT against about 6.8% before it.
  • Why supply shocks matter so much in India: food has a large weight in the CPI basket, so monsoon failures and vegetable price spikes (onions, tomatoes, pulses) are classic cost-push shocks. Interest-rate policy acts on demand, so it can do little about the first-round effect. The RBI's concern is the second-round effects: higher food prices feeding into wage demands and inflation expectations, the wage–price spiral of §1.4.

2.1 Demand, supply and what is held constant

The demand curve shows the quantity buyers are willing and able to buy at each price. The supply curve shows the quantity sellers are willing to offer at each price. Each curve is drawn ceteris paribus: everything except the good's own price is held fixed.

Key distinction: movement along vs shift of a curve

A change in the good's own price causes a movement along the curve: a change in quantity demanded or quantity supplied. A change in any shifter shifts the whole curve: a change in demand or supply. "Demand rose because the price fell" is wrong; the quantity demanded rose.

Behind the curves: demand comes from consumers' rational choice (Chapter 3) and supply from firms' costs of production (Chapter 4). The competitive firm's supply curve is its marginal cost curve above minimum AVC, so the market supply curve is the horizontal sum of the firms' MC curves.

2.2 Market equilibrium

Equilibrium is the price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. At that price there is no pressure to change. CORE (§8.2) notes that the competitive equilibrium is a Nash equilibrium: given what everyone else does, no buyer or seller can do better by acting differently.

Adjustment (the invisible hand's feedback loop from Welfare class 1): if the price is too high (P = 8), quantity supplied (6) exceeds quantity demanded (2). Unsold stock piles up and sellers cut prices. If the price is too low (P = 4), there is a shortage (Qd = 6 > Qs = 2). Buyers compete for the good and sellers raise prices. Price settles where demand = supply. No planner is needed; every participant reacts only to price.

Derivation: equilibrium in the general linear model

Let demand be \(Q_d = a - bP\) and supply \(Q_s = c + dP\), with \(a, b, d > 0\).

Set \(Q_d = Q_s\): \(a - bP = c + dP\).

Solve for price: \(a - c = (b + d)P\) ⇒ \(\boxed{P^* = \dfrac{a - c}{b + d}}\).

Substitute into either curve: \(Q^* = a - b\dfrac{a-c}{b+d} = \dfrac{a(b+d) - b(a-c)}{b+d}\) ⇒ \(\boxed{Q^* = \dfrac{ad + bc}{b + d}}\).

Check with the course example. \(P = 10 - Q\) means \(Q_d = 10 - P\) (\(a = 10, b = 1\)). \(P = 2 + Q\) means \(Q_s = -2 + P\) (\(c = -2, d = 1\)). \(P^* = 12/2 = 6\), \(Q^* = (10 - 2)/2 = 4\). ✓

2.3 Comparative statics: shifts in supply and demand

Derivation: comparative statics

From \(P^* = \dfrac{a-c}{b+d}\) and \(Q^* = \dfrac{ad+bc}{b+d}\):

  • Demand shift (\(a\) rises): \(\dfrac{\partial P^*}{\partial a} = \dfrac{1}{b+d} > 0\) and \(\dfrac{\partial Q^*}{\partial a} = \dfrac{d}{b+d} > 0\). Both rise.
  • Supply shift (\(c\) rises, i.e. supply increases): \(\dfrac{\partial P^*}{\partial c} = \dfrac{-1}{b+d} < 0\) and \(\dfrac{\partial Q^*}{\partial c} = \dfrac{b}{b+d} > 0\). Price falls, quantity rises.
  • The split between price and quantity depends on slopes. A demand shift raises \(Q\) a lot and \(P\) a little when supply is flat (large \(d\)), and the reverse when supply is steep. Hence the importance of elasticity.

2.4 Price elasticity of demand

Slope depends on the units of measurement; elasticity does not. The price elasticity of demand is the percentage change in quantity demanded caused by a 1% change in price:

$$\varepsilon_d = \frac{\%\Delta Q_d}{\%\Delta P} = \frac{\Delta Q / Q}{\Delta P / P} = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta P}\cdot\frac{P}{Q}\quad\xrightarrow{\text{point}}\quad \varepsilon_d = \frac{dQ}{dP}\cdot\frac{P}{Q}.$$

It is negative for a downward-sloping demand curve. Books (and CORE) usually quote the absolute value \(|\varepsilon|\) or define \(\varepsilon = -\frac{dQ}{dP}\frac{P}{Q} > 0\).

Midpoint (arc) formula (Goodwin Box 5.2). It gives the same answer whichever direction the price moves:

$$\varepsilon = \frac{(Q_2 - Q_1)/\big[(Q_1+Q_2)/2\big]}{(P_2 - P_1)/\big[(P_1+P_2)/2\big]}.$$
Worked example

Gym memberships fall from 100 to 80 when the monthly fee rises from ₹1,000 to ₹1,200. Midpoint: \(\%\Delta Q = -20/90 = -22.2\%\) and \(\%\Delta P = 200/1100 = 18.2\%\), so \(\varepsilon = -1.22\): elastic. (Using the start point instead gives \(-20\% / 20\% = -1.0\), which is why the midpoint formula is preferred.)

Elasticity along a linear demand curve

Derivation: elasticity varies along a straight line

Linear demand \(Q = a - bP\), so \(dQ/dP = -b\) (constant slope).

\(\varepsilon = -b\cdot\dfrac{P}{Q} = \dfrac{-bP}{a - bP}\).

At the vertical intercept (\(Q = 0\)), \(|\varepsilon| \to \infty\). At the horizontal intercept (\(P = 0\)), \(\varepsilon = 0\).

Unit elasticity: \(bP = a - bP\) ⇒ \(P = \dfrac{a}{2b}\) and \(Q = \dfrac{a}{2}\): the midpoint of the line. Above it demand is elastic; below it, inelastic.

Lesson: a straight-line demand curve does not have constant elasticity. Only \(Q = AP^{-\eta}\) (a constant-elasticity curve) has the same elasticity \(\eta\) everywhere.

Elasticity and total revenue

Derivation: how revenue responds to price

Total revenue (= consumers' expenditure) \(TR = P\cdot Q(P)\).

Product rule: \(\dfrac{dTR}{dP} = Q + P\dfrac{dQ}{dP} = Q\left(1 + \dfrac{P}{Q}\dfrac{dQ}{dP}\right) = Q(1 + \varepsilon) = Q(1 - |\varepsilon|)\).

So: if \(|\varepsilon| < 1\) (inelastic), \(dTR/dP > 0\): raising price raises revenue. If \(|\varepsilon| > 1\) (elastic), raising price lowers revenue. If \(|\varepsilon| = 1\), revenue is at its maximum.

Equivalently, in terms of quantity: \(MR = \dfrac{dTR}{dQ} = P + Q\dfrac{dP}{dQ} = P\left(1 - \dfrac{1}{|\varepsilon|}\right)\). This formula is central to monopoly (Chapter 5).

2.5 Income, cross-price and supply elasticities

Income elasticity of demand

$$\eta_M = \frac{\%\Delta Q}{\%\Delta M} = \frac{dQ}{dM}\cdot\frac{M}{Q}.$$
  • \(\eta_M > 0\): normal good. If \(0 < \eta_M < 1\) it is a necessity (food, so the budget share of food falls as income rises: Engel's law). If \(\eta_M > 1\) it is a luxury (air travel, eating out).
  • \(\eta_M < 0\): inferior good. Demand falls as income rises (coarse grains, bus travel replaced by cars as households get richer).

Cross-price elasticity

$$\varepsilon_{xy} = \frac{\%\Delta Q_x}{\%\Delta P_y}:\qquad \varepsilon_{xy} > 0 \text{ substitutes},\quad \varepsilon_{xy} < 0 \text{ complements},\quad \varepsilon_{xy} = 0 \text{ unrelated}.$$

Price elasticity of supply

$$\varepsilon_s = \frac{\%\Delta Q_s}{\%\Delta P} \ge 0.$$

Supply is less elastic in the short run, when capacity, land and plant are fixed (a farmer cannot grow more onions until the next season), and more elastic in the long run, when firms can expand, build plant and enter. Supply of goods using specialised or scarce inputs (land in central Mumbai, Darjeeling tea) stays inelastic even in the long run.

Worked example: point elasticities from a demand function

\(Q_x = 120 - 4P_x + 2P_y + 0.01M\), with \(P_x = 10\), \(P_y = 20\), \(M = 2000\). Then \(Q_x = 120 - 40 + 40 + 20 = 140\).

  • Own price: \(\varepsilon = -4 \times 10/140 = -0.29\) (inelastic).
  • Cross price: \(\varepsilon_{xy} = 2 \times 20/140 = 0.29 > 0\): x and y are substitutes.
  • Income: \(\eta_M = 0.01 \times 2000/140 = 0.14\): a normal good and a necessity.

2.6 Who bears a tax? Elasticity and incidence

Derivation: tax incidence with linear curves

A per-unit tax \(\tau\) is levied on sellers. Buyers pay \(P_b\); sellers receive \(P_s = P_b - \tau\).

Equilibrium: \(a - bP_b = c + d(P_b - \tau)\) ⇒ \(P_b = \dfrac{a - c + d\tau}{b + d} = P^* + \dfrac{d}{b+d}\tau\).

\(P_s = P_b - \tau = P^* - \dfrac{b}{b+d}\tau\).

Buyers' share of the tax is \(\dfrac{d}{b+d}\); sellers' share is \(\dfrac{b}{b+d}\). Dividing, and using the fact that at the initial point \(\varepsilon_s/|\varepsilon_d| = d/b\): $$\frac{\text{buyers' burden}}{\text{sellers' burden}} = \frac{d}{b} = \frac{\varepsilon_s}{|\varepsilon_d|}.$$

The less elastic side bears more of the tax, whoever legally pays it. Course example: \(b = d = 1\), \(\tau = 2\): buyers pay 1 more (6 → 7) and sellers receive 1 less (6 → 5). This is why taxes on cigarettes, alcohol and fuel (inelastic demand) fall mainly on consumers. Welfare effects are in Chapter 7.

2.7 Macro application: employment elasticity

The employment elasticity of output measures how responsive jobs are to economic growth:

$$e_{N} = \frac{\%\Delta \text{Employment}}{\%\Delta \text{GDP}}.$$

With \(e_N = 0.2\) and 7% GDP growth, employment grows only 1.4% a year. Since employment = output / labour productivity, \(\hat N = \hat Y - \hat\lambda\) and so \(e_N = 1 - \hat\lambda/\hat Y\). A low elasticity means growth comes mostly from rising productivity (capital-intensive sectors) rather than from more jobs.

India focus: "jobless growth"

Indian studies find low employment elasticities, especially in manufacturing and organised services. In some periods of fast GDP growth (notably the mid-2000s to early 2010s) estimates were close to zero, which gave rise to the phrase jobless growth. Agriculture still employs a large share of workers but has low productivity, so moving labour into industry and services that create jobs is a key policy concern.

3.1 How do consumers value goods?

Classical economists (Smith, Ricardo, Marx) distinguished use-value (usefulness) from exchange-value (price), and explained exchange-value by costs of production, ultimately labour. Smith's diamond–water paradox asked why water, very useful, is cheap while diamonds, nearly useless, are dear.

The marginalist revolution of the 1870s (Jevons, Menger, Walras) answered: value depends on marginal utility, the extra satisfaction from one more unit, not total utility. Water is abundant, so the marginal glass adds little; diamonds are scarce, so the marginal diamond adds a lot.

Key ideas
  • Total utility \(U(x)\); marginal utility \(MU_x = \partial U/\partial x\).
  • Law of diminishing marginal utility: each extra unit adds less satisfaction than the last (\(\partial^2 U/\partial x^2 < 0\)). The fifth samosa is enjoyed less than the first.
  • Marginalist calculus: do more of an activity as long as its marginal benefit exceeds its marginal cost, and stop where MB = MC. This one rule underlies consumer choice (MU per rupee), firm choice (MR = MC) and efficiency (marginal social benefit = marginal social cost).

Total utility rises at the same total spending, so the original bundle was not optimal. Repeat the argument the other way. The only bundle where no reallocation helps (with both goods bought and the budget spent) satisfies $$\boxed{\,\frac{MU_x}{P_x} = \frac{MU_y}{P_y}\,}\quad\text{and}\quad P_x x + P_y y = M.$$

3.2 Choice and rationality: preferences

Modern theory needs only that consumers can rank bundles (ordinal utility). A preference ordering is rational if it satisfies:

  1. Completeness: for any two bundles A and B, either A ≽ B, or B ≽ A, or both (indifference). The consumer can always compare.
  2. Transitivity: if A ≽ B and B ≽ C, then A ≽ C. This rules out cycles that could be exploited ("money pump").

To formulate the choice problem we add:

  1. Monotonicity (non-satiation): more is better. A bundle with more of at least one good and no less of any other is preferred.
  2. Convexity: averages are preferred to extremes (diminishing MRS). This makes indifference curves bowed toward the origin.

If preferences are complete, transitive (and continuous), they can be represented by a utility function \(U(x,y)\). Any increasing transformation of \(U\) represents the same preferences: only the ranking matters.

3.3 The price line (budget constraint)

With income \(M\) and prices \(P_x, P_y\), the consumer can afford bundles satisfying \(P_x x + P_y y \le M\). The budget line is the boundary:

$$y = \frac{M}{P_y} - \frac{P_x}{P_y}\,x.$$
  • Intercepts: \(M/P_y\) (all on y) and \(M/P_x\) (all on x).
  • Slope = \(-P_x/P_y\): the market rate at which y can be traded for x, the opportunity cost of x in units of y. CORE calls this the marginal rate of transformation (MRT) (§3.5).
  • An income rise shifts the line out in parallel. A fall in \(P_x\) pivots it outward around the y-intercept.

3.4 Indifference curves and the MRS

An indifference curve joins all bundles giving the same utility. Properties under the assumptions above:

  • Downward sloping (monotonicity): to keep utility constant, more x requires less y.
  • Higher curves = higher utility (monotonicity).
  • Never cross. Proof: if two curves crossed at bundle C, then A ~ C (on curve 1) and C ~ B (on curve 2), so A ~ B by transitivity. But one of A and B has more of both goods, which contradicts monotonicity.
  • Convex to the origin: the MRS diminishes as x increases.
Derivation: the slope of an indifference curve

Along an indifference curve utility is constant: \(dU = \dfrac{\partial U}{\partial x}dx + \dfrac{\partial U}{\partial y}dy = 0\).

Rearrange: \(\left.\dfrac{dy}{dx}\right|_{U=\bar U} = -\dfrac{MU_x}{MU_y}\).

Define the marginal rate of substitution \(MRS_{xy} = -\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{MU_x}{MU_y}\): how many units of y the consumer is willing to give up for one more unit of x (CORE §3.3).

Example: \(U = xy\) gives \(MU_x = y\), \(MU_y = x\), so \(MRS = y/x\). This falls as x rises along a curve: diminishing MRS, hence convexity.

3.5 Consumer equilibrium

Intuitive meaning. At the optimum the rate at which the consumer is willing to swap goods (MRS) equals the rate at which the market lets them swap (price ratio):

$$\boxed{\,MRS_{xy} = \frac{MU_x}{MU_y} = \frac{P_x}{P_y}\,}\qquad (\text{CORE: } MRS = MRT).$$

If \(MRS > P_x/P_y\) (point F: the indifference curve is steeper than the budget line), the consumer values x more than the market charges for it, so buying more x raises utility. If \(MRS < P_x/P_y\), buy less x. Rearranged, the tangency condition is the equimarginal principle: \(MU_x/P_x = MU_y/P_y\).

Derivation: the Lagrangian (mathematical meaning)

Problem: \(\max_{x,y}\, U(x,y)\) subject to \(P_x x + P_y y = M\).

Lagrangian: \(\mathcal L = U(x,y) + \lambda\,(M - P_x x - P_y y)\).

First-order conditions: $$\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial x} = MU_x - \lambda P_x = 0,\quad \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial y} = MU_y - \lambda P_y = 0,\quad \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\lambda} = M - P_x x - P_y y = 0.$$

From the first two: \(\lambda = \dfrac{MU_x}{P_x} = \dfrac{MU_y}{P_y}\), so \(\dfrac{MU_x}{MU_y} = \dfrac{P_x}{P_y}\). \(\lambda\) is the marginal utility of income: the extra utility from one more rupee.

The second-order condition holds when indifference curves are strictly convex, so the tangency is a maximum, not a minimum.

Derivation: Cobb-Douglas demand functions

Let \(U = x^\alpha y^{1-\alpha}\), \(0 < \alpha < 1\).

\(MU_x = \alpha x^{\alpha-1}y^{1-\alpha} = \alpha U/x\) and \(MU_y = (1-\alpha)U/y\), so \(MRS = \dfrac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\cdot\dfrac{y}{x}\).

Tangency: \(\dfrac{\alpha y}{(1-\alpha)x} = \dfrac{P_x}{P_y}\) ⇒ \(P_y y = \dfrac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}P_x x\).

Substitute into the budget: \(P_x x + \dfrac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}P_x x = M\) ⇒ \(\dfrac{P_x x}{\alpha} = M\).

Demands: \(\boxed{x^* = \dfrac{\alpha M}{P_x},\quad y^* = \dfrac{(1-\alpha)M}{P_y}}\). The consumer spends a constant share \(\alpha\) of income on x.

Example (figure above): \(U = xy\) is the case \(\alpha = \tfrac12\) (up to a monotone transformation). With \(M = 80\), \(P_x = 4\), \(P_y = 1\): \(x^* = 40/4 = 10\), \(y^* = 40\), \(U = 400\). Check: \(MRS = y/x = 4 = P_x/P_y\). ✓

Corner solutions. If even at \(x = 0\) we have \(MRS < P_x/P_y\), the consumer buys none of x. This is common for goods we never buy, and the tangency condition does not apply there.

3.6 Changes in price and income

When \(P_x\) falls, the total change in quantity (the price effect) splits into:

  • the substitution effect (SE): x is now relatively cheaper, so the consumer substitutes toward it, holding utility constant (a movement along the original indifference curve). With convex preferences the SE of a price fall is always positive for x.
  • the income effect (IE): the lower price raises real income (purchasing power), a parallel shift. For a normal good this raises x; for an inferior good it lowers x.
Derivation: decomposing the price effect (numbers in the figure)

Original: \(P_x = 4\): \(x = M/(2P_x) = 10\), \(y = 40\), \(U_0 = 400\). (Point A)

New: \(P_x = 1\): \(x = 80/2 = 40\), \(y = 40\), \(U_1 = 1600\). (Point B) Total price effect = 40 − 10 = +30.

Compensated bundle C: the cheapest bundle at the new prices that still gives \(U_0 = 400\). Tangency at new prices: \(y/x = P_x/P_y = 1\) ⇒ \(y = x\). On \(U_0\): \(x^2 = 400\) ⇒ \(x = 20\), \(y = 20\). The compensated income is \(1(20) + 1(20) = 40\), so the consumer could lose ₹40 and be as well off as before.

SE = 20 − 10 = +10; IE = 40 − 20 = +20; SE + IE = 30 = total. ✓

Slutsky alternative: compensate to keep the old bundle affordable instead: \(M' = 1(10) + 1(40) = 50\), so \(x = 50/2 = 25\). SE = 15, IE = 15. The signs are the same, but the split differs.

Conditions for a Giffen good: (1) the good is inferior; (2) it takes a large share of the budget, so price changes have big income effects; (3) there are few close substitutes. Classic cases are staple foods for very poor households: Jensen and Miller (2008) found Giffen behaviour for rice among poor households in Hunan, China. When rice gets cheaper, families can afford more meat and buy less rice.

Advanced: the Slutsky equation

Let \(x(P_x, M)\) be ordinary (Marshallian) demand and \(h(P_x, \bar U)\) compensated (Hicksian) demand. Then $$\frac{\partial x}{\partial P_x} = \underbrace{\frac{\partial h}{\partial P_x}}_{\text{SE}\,\le\,0} \; - \; \underbrace{x\,\frac{\partial x}{\partial M}}_{\text{IE}}.$$ For a normal good \(\partial x/\partial M > 0\), so both terms are negative and demand slopes down. A Giffen good needs \(x\,\partial x/\partial M\) to be negative and large: strongly inferior, with x large (a big budget share).

Deriving the demand curve

Vary \(P_x\), holding \(M\), \(P_y\) and preferences fixed. Record the optimal x each time. Plotting the optimal points in (x, y) space traces the price-consumption curve (PCC). Plotting (x, \(P_x\)) gives the individual demand curve. The market demand curve is the horizontal sum of individual demands.

Varying income instead traces the income-consumption curve and the Engel curve (x against M). It slopes up for normal goods and down for inferior goods.

3.7 Labour–leisure choice

The same tools explain how many hours people choose to work (CORE Unit 3). A person has \(N\) hours (e.g. 24 per day), chooses leisure \(l\) and works \(N - l\) hours at wage \(w\), spending the earnings on consumption \(C\) (all other goods):

$$\max_{l,\,C}\; U(l, C)\quad \text{subject to}\quad C = w(N - l).$$
  • The budget line \(C = wN - wl\) has slope \(-w\): the wage is the opportunity cost (price) of an hour of leisure. CORE calls this the MRT between free time and goods.
  • Its intercepts are \(wN\) (no leisure) and \(N\) (no work, no consumption).
Derivation: optimal hours

Lagrangian: \(\mathcal L = U(l, C) + \lambda\,[\,w(N-l) - C\,]\).

FOCs: \(U_l = \lambda w\) and \(U_C = \lambda\) ⇒ \(\boxed{MRS_{lC} = \dfrac{U_l}{U_C} = w}\). The value of the last hour of leisure, in goods, equals the goods that hour of work would buy.

Cobb-Douglas \(U = l^\alpha C^{1-\alpha}\): \(\dfrac{\alpha C}{(1-\alpha) l} = w\) ⇒ \(C = \dfrac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}wl\). Substitute into \(C = w(N-l)\): \(\dfrac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}wl = wN - wl\) ⇒ \(\dfrac{wl}{\alpha} = wN\) ⇒ \(\boxed{l^* = \alpha N}\).

Here leisure, and so hours worked \(h = (1-\alpha)N\), do not depend on the wage: the substitution and income effects exactly cancel. With \(\alpha = \tfrac12\), \(N = 24\): \(l^* = 12\), and \(C = 12w\) (₹60 at w = 5, ₹120 at w = 10). See the figure.

Why income and substitution effects work in opposite directions here

For an ordinary good, a fall in its price has a substitution and an income effect that both raise demand (if normal). For leisure, a rise in the wage is a rise in the price of leisure, but it also makes the worker richer because they sell labour:

  • SE: leisure is now more expensive (each hour off costs more goods), so the worker takes less leisure and works more.
  • IE: at the same hours the worker earns more. If leisure is a normal good they want more leisure and work less.

The net effect on hours is ambiguous (CORE §3.8). At low wages the SE usually dominates and labour supply slopes upward. At high wages, where income is already high, the IE can dominate: higher wages lead to fewer hours. The labour supply curve then bends backward.

Derivation: a backward-bending case (the "target earner")

Suppose the worker needs a subsistence level of consumption \(\bar C\) and has \(U = \alpha\ln l + (1-\alpha)\ln(C - \bar C)\).

Let \(C' = C - \bar C\). The budget becomes \(C' = wN - \bar C - wl\), so "full income" is \(wN - \bar C\).

By the Cobb-Douglas result, spending on leisure is the share \(\alpha\) of full income: \(wl^* = \alpha(wN - \bar C)\) ⇒ \(l^* = \alpha\left(N - \dfrac{\bar C}{w}\right)\).

Hours: \(h^* = N - l^* = (1-\alpha)N + \dfrac{\alpha\bar C}{w}\). Then \(\dfrac{dh^*}{dw} = -\dfrac{\alpha\bar C}{w^2} < 0\): hours fall as the wage rises.

Numbers: \(N = 16\) waking hours, \(\alpha = \tfrac12\), \(\bar C = ₹120\). At \(w = 10\): \(h = 8 + 6 = 14\) hours; at \(w = 20\): 11 hours; at \(w = 60\): 9 hours. A worker who must first earn the subsistence ₹120 works long hours at low wages and cuts back as wages rise.

Evidence and applications
  • CORE §3.10: in the US (1900–2013), as wages rose, people took more free time and more goods, so the income effect dominated over the long run. But in Britain between 1600 and 1870, working days per year rose from about 266 to 318 as wages rose, so the substitution effect dominated.
  • Backward-bending supply is often discussed for agricultural and informal workers in South Asia who have consumption targets. It also matters for taxation: a higher income tax lowers the net wage, and whether people then work more or less depends on which effect dominates.
  • CORE §3.11 extends the model to women's labour force participation, which is low in India. Social norms and unpaid care work change the effective "price" of time and the options available.

3.8 Limits of the standard consumer model

Goodwin et al. (Ch. 9) caution that the model assumes consumer sovereignty (preferences are the consumer's own and given). In practice preferences are shaped by social comparison (conspicuous consumption, "keeping up with the Joneses"), advertising and habit; consumption has environmental costs that buyers do not pay (externalities, Chapter 7); and beyond a point more income and consumption add little to reported happiness. Behavioural economics also documents departures from transitivity and full rationality. Treat the model as a useful benchmark, not a literal description.

4.1 The firm's problem

Traditional microeconomics assumes the firm's objective is profit maximisation. To understand how output is chosen we need two building blocks:

Key ideas: the two pillars
  • Production function \(Q = f(L, K)\): the maximum output obtainable from each combination of inputs, given technology. It is a technical relationship.
  • Cost function \(C(Q)\): the minimum cost of producing each output level, given input prices. It is derived from the production function plus input prices.
  • Profit: \(\pi(Q) = TR(Q) - TC(Q)\).

4.2 Concepts of cost

  • Fixed costs (FC) do not vary with output in the short run: rent, machinery, insurance. Sunk costs are fixed costs that cannot be recovered even by shutting down, so they should not affect current decisions (Goodwin Box 17.2).
  • Variable costs (VC) rise with output: raw materials, hourly labour, power.
  • Short run vs long run. In the short run at least one input (usually capital, \(\bar K\)) is fixed. The long run is a period long enough that all inputs, and so all costs, are variable. There are no fixed costs in the long run. (These are defined by flexibility, not calendar time.)
  • Accounting vs economic cost. Accounting costs are explicit money payments. Economic cost adds opportunity costs: the value of the best alternative use of the firm's own scarce inputs, such as the owner's time and capital.
Worked example: accounting vs economic profit

A tea stall earns revenue of ₹6,00,000 a year and pays ₹3,50,000 in explicit costs, so accounting profit is ₹2,50,000. The owner could earn ₹2,00,000 as a salaried employee, and the ₹5,00,000 of savings in the business could earn 8% (₹40,000) in a bank. Economic profit = 2,50,000 − 2,00,000 − 40,000 = ₹10,000. Zero economic profit means earning exactly normal profit: just enough to keep resources in their current use.

Goodwin et al. also distinguish private costs (paid by the firm) from external costs (pollution borne by others). That distinction drives Chapter 7.

4.3 Production in the short run

With capital fixed, output varies only with labour: \(Q = f(L, \bar K)\).

  • Marginal product \(MP_L = \dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial L}\): the extra output from one more worker.
  • Average product \(AP_L = \dfrac{Q}{L}\).
  • Law of diminishing marginal returns: as more of a variable input is added to a fixed input, MP eventually falls. MP can rise at first (specialisation), then fall, and eventually become negative (too many workers crowding a fixed kitchen).
Derivation: MP cuts AP at the maximum of AP

\(\dfrac{d\,AP}{dL} = \dfrac{d}{dL}\left(\dfrac{Q}{L}\right) = \dfrac{L\cdot MP - Q}{L^2} = \dfrac{1}{L}\left(MP - AP\right)\).

So AP is rising when MP > AP, falling when MP < AP, and at a stationary point (maximum) when MP = AP. (Marginal-average logic: if your next exam mark exceeds your average, your average rises.)

Check: \(AP = 6L - 0.4L^2\) has its maximum where \(6 - 0.8L = 0\), at L = 7.5. There \(AP = 45 - 22.5 = 22.5\) and \(MP = 90 - 67.5 = 22.5\). ✓

4.4 Returns to scale (the long run)

Returns to a factor (MP) is a short-run idea: one input varies. Returns to scale is a long-run idea: all inputs vary together. Multiply all inputs by \(\lambda > 1\):

If \(f(\lambda L, \lambda K)\) is…then there are…Typical cause
\(> \lambda f(L,K)\)Increasing returns to scale (IRS)Specialisation, indivisible equipment, spreading design costs, geometry (a pipe twice as wide carries four times as much)
\(= \lambda f(L,K)\)Constant returns to scale (CRS)Replicating a plant
\(< \lambda f(L,K)\)Decreasing returns to scale (DRS)Managerial and coordination problems in very large organisations
Derivation: returns to scale of a Cobb-Douglas function

\(Q = AL^aK^b\). Then \(f(\lambda L, \lambda K) = A(\lambda L)^a(\lambda K)^b = \lambda^{a+b}AL^aK^b = \lambda^{a+b}Q\).

So the function shows IRS if \(a + b > 1\), CRS if \(a + b = 1\) and DRS if \(a + b < 1\). Note that \(Q = L^{0.7}K^{0.7}\) has diminishing returns to each factor (\(MP_L \propto L^{-0.3}\) falls as L rises) and increasing returns to scale (\(0.7 + 0.7 = 1.4\)). The two ideas are independent: do not confuse them.

Derivation: cost curves mirror product curves

In the short run with one variable input, \(VC = wL\), where \(w\) is the wage.

\(MC = \dfrac{dVC}{dQ} = w\dfrac{dL}{dQ} = \dfrac{w}{dQ/dL} = \boxed{\dfrac{w}{MP_L}}\).

\(AVC = \dfrac{wL}{Q} = \boxed{\dfrac{w}{AP_L}}\).

So when MP is rising, MC is falling; diminishing marginal returns cause rising marginal cost. Max MP corresponds to min MC, and max AP to min AVC. The U-shapes of the cost curves are the product curves turned upside down.

Derivation: MC passes through the minimum of ATC (and of AVC)

\(\dfrac{d\,ATC}{dQ} = \dfrac{d}{dQ}\left(\dfrac{TC}{Q}\right) = \dfrac{Q\cdot MC - TC}{Q^2} = \dfrac{1}{Q}\left(MC - ATC\right)\).

ATC falls when MC < ATC, rises when MC > ATC, and is at its minimum where MC = ATC. The identical argument with VC gives MC = AVC at min AVC.

Since ATC = AVC + AFC and AFC keeps falling, min ATC occurs at a larger output than min AVC.

Worked example: cubic costs

\(TC = 60 + 12Q - 2Q^2 + 0.2Q^3\): FC = 60, \(VC = 12Q - 2Q^2 + 0.2Q^3\).

  • \(MC = 12 - 4Q + 0.6Q^2\). Its minimum is where \(-4 + 1.2Q = 0\): Q = 3.33, MC = 5.33.
  • \(AVC = 12 - 2Q + 0.2Q^2\). Its minimum is where \(-2 + 0.4Q = 0\): Q = 5, AVC = 7. Check MC(5) = 12 − 20 + 15 = 7 ✓.
  • \(ATC = 60/Q + AVC\). Minimum where \(-60/Q^2 - 2 + 0.4Q = 0\), i.e. \(Q^3 - 5Q^2 - 150 = 0\): Q ≈ 7.6, ATC ≈ 16.2.

Costs in the long run and economies of scale

In the long run the firm chooses its plant size. The long-run average cost (LRAC) curve is the lower envelope of all the short-run ATC curves: for each output, the lowest average cost over all plant sizes. Its shape reflects returns to scale: falling with IRS (economies of scale), flat with CRS, rising with DRS (diseconomies of scale). The output at which LRAC first reaches its minimum is the minimum efficient scale (MES).

4.6 Profit maximisation: MR = MC

Derivation: the profit-maximising rule

\(\pi(Q) = TR(Q) - TC(Q)\).

First-order condition: \(\dfrac{d\pi}{dQ} = MR(Q) - MC(Q) = 0\) ⇒ \(\boxed{MR = MC}\).

Second-order condition: \(\dfrac{d^2\pi}{dQ^2} = \dfrac{dMR}{dQ} - \dfrac{dMC}{dQ} < 0\). The MC curve must cut MR from below (MC rising relative to MR).

Intuition (CORE §7.5): if MR > MC, one more unit adds more to revenue than to cost, so expand. If MR < MC, contract. Profit peaks where they are equal.

What MR looks like depends on market structure: for a price-taker MR = P (this chapter); for a firm facing a downward-sloping demand curve MR < P (Chapter 5).

4.7 Perfect competition

Conditions of perfect competition (the benchmark market)
  1. Large numbers of buyers and sellers, each too small to affect the price: all are price-takers.
  2. Free entry and exit for producers.
  3. Identical (homogeneous) products.
  4. Perfect and symmetric information: everyone knows prices and quality.

Agricultural commodity markets and some financial markets come close; Goodwin et al. (§17.2) note that true examples are rare.

Derivation: P = AR = MR for a price-taker

The firm can sell any quantity at the market price \(P\), so it faces a perfectly elastic (horizontal) demand curve. Then \(TR = P\cdot q\), \(AR = TR/q = P\) and \(MR = dTR/dq = P\). The profit-maximising condition becomes $$\boxed{\,P = MC(q)\,}\quad\text{on the rising part of MC.}$$

4.8 Short-run equilibrium of the competitive firm

In the short run the price can be above ATC (supernormal or economic profit), equal to it (normal profit), or below it (losses). Profit per unit is \(P - ATC\), so total profit is \((P - ATC)\times q\).

Should a loss-making firm keep producing? The shutdown condition

This answers the lecture's question: "Will the competitive firm be producing output if its average cost is not covered by the price?"

Derivation: shutdown rule

If the firm shuts down in the short run, it still pays fixed costs: \(\pi_{\text{shut}} = -FC\).

If it produces q: \(\pi = Pq - VC(q) - FC\).

Produce if \(Pq - VC - FC \ge -FC\) ⇔ \(Pq \ge VC\) ⇔ \(\boxed{P \ge AVC(q)}\).

So the firm produces, even at a loss, as long as price covers average variable cost: revenue then pays all variable costs and contributes something toward fixed costs. It shuts down if \(P < \min AVC\).

Answer: yes. If \(\min AVC \le P < \min ATC\) the firm makes a loss but keeps producing in the short run, because the loss is smaller than FC. In the long run, when nothing is fixed, it exits unless \(P \ge \min ATC\).

Worked example: firm and market supply

Cubic firm above: supply \(q(P)\) solves \(P = 12 - 4q + 0.6q^2\) on the rising part, for \(P \ge 7\); \(q = 0\) for \(P < 7\).

  • P = 20: q ≈ 8.28, ATC ≈ 16.4: profit ≈ +₹30.
  • P = 12: \(0.6q^2 - 4q = 0\) ⇒ q = 6.67, AVC = 12 − 13.3 + 8.9 = 7.6 < 12 < ATC = 16.6. Loss = (12 − 16.6)(6.67) ≈ −₹30.5, which is smaller than FC = 60, so it keeps producing.
  • P = 6 < min AVC = 7: shut down; loss = FC = ₹60.

With 100 identical firms, market supply is \(Q = 100\,q(P)\): the horizontal sum of the firms' MC curves.

4.9 Long-run equilibrium

In the long run, firms can enter or exit and adjust plant size:

  • If existing firms earn supernormal profit, new firms are lured in. Market supply shifts right and price falls.
  • If firms make losses, some exit. Supply shifts left and price rises.
  • Entry and exit stop when economic profit is zero: \(\boxed{P = MC = \min LRAC}\). Each firm earns only normal profit and produces at minimum efficient scale.
Derivation: long-run equilibrium (numbers in the figure)

Each firm: \(TC = 100 + 4q + q^2\). Market demand: \(Q_d = 1000 - 20P\).

With identical firms and constant input prices (a constant-cost industry), the long-run industry supply curve is horizontal at P = 24. A demand increase raises the number of firms, not the long-run price.

Why perfect competition is the benchmark

  • Allocative efficiency: \(P = MC\). The value consumers place on the last unit equals the cost of producing it, so total surplus is maximised (Chapter 7; CORE §8.4).
  • Productive efficiency: in the long run \(P = \min LRAC\). Output is produced at the lowest possible cost.
  • Goodwin et al. (§17.5) add caveats: real markets show path dependence and network externalities (the QWERTY keyboard), and efficiency says nothing about equity.

Chapter 5Monopoly and Price Discrimination

A single price-setting seller: why marginal revenue lies below price, how the monopolist chooses output and price, why this is inefficient, and how price discrimination changes the picture.

Readings for this chapter
  • Goodwin et al., Principles of Economics in Context, Ch. 18 (Markets with Market Power), §2 (monopoly).
  • CORE The Economy: South Asia, Unit 7: §7.2–7.5 (demand, isoprofit curves, MR and MC), §7.6 (gains from trade and deadweight loss), §7.7 (elasticity and the markup), §7.9 (market power and policy). The Leibniz page for §7.8.1 gives the calculus of the markup rule.

5.1 Conditions of pure monopoly

Key ideas
  1. One seller of the product.
  2. No close substitutes.
  3. Strong barriers to entry: legal (patents, licences, state monopoly such as Indian Railways passenger services), control of an essential resource, economies of scale so large that one firm can supply the market more cheaply than two (a natural monopoly: electricity grids, water pipes, metro rail), and network effects.

Together these make the firm a price-maker: it faces the downward-sloping market demand curve and chooses a point on it.

  • There is no distinction between firm and industry equilibrium: the firm is the industry.
  • Because entry is blocked, supernormal profit can persist: there is no separate short-run and long-run equilibrium analysis of the kind used for perfect competition.
  • A monopolist has no supply curve: the quantity it supplies at a given price depends on the shape of demand, not only on MC.

5.2 Marginal revenue under monopoly

To sell one more unit the monopolist must lower the price on all units (no price discrimination). So MR has two parts: the price of the extra unit (gain) minus the revenue lost on the units it was already selling (loss).

Derivation: \(MR = P\left(1 - \dfrac{1}{|\varepsilon|}\right)\)

Inverse demand \(P = P(Q)\), with \(P'(Q) < 0\). Revenue: \(TR = P(Q)\cdot Q\).

\(MR = \dfrac{dTR}{dQ} = P + Q\,\dfrac{dP}{dQ}\). The first term is the gain from the extra unit; the second (negative) term is the loss on the earlier units.

Factor out P: \(MR = P\left(1 + \dfrac{Q}{P}\dfrac{dP}{dQ}\right)\). Since \(\varepsilon = \dfrac{dQ}{dP}\dfrac{P}{Q}\), we have \(\dfrac{Q}{P}\dfrac{dP}{dQ} = \dfrac{1}{\varepsilon} = -\dfrac{1}{|\varepsilon|}\).

\(\boxed{MR = P\left(1 - \dfrac{1}{|\varepsilon|}\right)}\)

Implications: \(MR < P\) for any downward-sloping demand. \(MR > 0\) only when \(|\varepsilon| > 1\). Since \(MC > 0\) and the monopolist sets \(MR = MC\), it always produces on the elastic part of the demand curve.

Derivation: linear demand

\(P = a - bQ\) ⇒ \(TR = aQ - bQ^2\) ⇒ \(MR = a - 2bQ\). The MR line has the same intercept as demand and twice the slope, so it cuts the quantity axis at \(a/(2b)\), halfway to demand's intercept. That is the unit-elastic midpoint, where TR is at its maximum.

5.3 Profit maximisation and equilibrium

Derivation: monopoly equilibrium with linear demand and constant MC

Demand \(P = a - bQ\), constant marginal and average cost \(c\) (so \(TC = cQ\)).

\(\pi = (a - bQ)Q - cQ\). FOC: \(a - 2bQ - c = 0\) (i.e. MR = MC).

\(Q_m = \dfrac{a - c}{2b}\); the price is read off the demand curve: \(P_m = a - bQ_m = \dfrac{a + c}{2}\).

Profit: \(\pi_m = (P_m - c)Q_m = \dfrac{a-c}{2}\cdot\dfrac{a-c}{2b} = \dfrac{(a-c)^2}{4b}\).

Compare perfect competition (\(P = MC = c\)): \(Q_c = \dfrac{a-c}{b} = 2Q_m\). The monopolist produces half the competitive output (for linear demand and constant MC) and charges a higher price.

5.4 The markup and the Lerner index

Derivation: the inverse-elasticity markup rule (CORE §7.7)

MR = MC ⇒ \(P\left(1 - \dfrac{1}{|\varepsilon|}\right) = MC\).

Rearrange: \(P - MC = \dfrac{P}{|\varepsilon|}\) ⇒ \(\boxed{\dfrac{P - MC}{P} = \dfrac{1}{|\varepsilon|}}\). The left side is the Lerner index of market power, the price–cost margin as a share of price.

Equivalently \(P = \dfrac{MC}{1 - 1/|\varepsilon|} = \dfrac{|\varepsilon|}{|\varepsilon| - 1}\,MC\).

The markup is inversely proportional to the elasticity of demand. CORE's examples: \(|\varepsilon| = 5\) gives a markup of 20% of price; \(|\varepsilon| = 1.25\) gives 80%. In the worked example, at the monopoly point \(|\varepsilon| = P/(bQ) = 60/40 = 1.5\), so the Lerner index = \((60-20)/60 = 2/3 = 1/1.5\). ✓ Under perfect competition \(|\varepsilon| \to \infty\), the Lerner index is 0 and P = MC.

5.5 Inefficiency of monopoly

Derivation: deadweight loss with linear demand

DWL is the triangle between demand and MC from \(Q_m\) to \(Q_c\): $$DWL = \tfrac12\,(P_m - c)(Q_c - Q_m) = \tfrac12\cdot\frac{a-c}{2}\cdot\frac{a-c}{2b} = \frac{(a-c)^2}{8b}.$$ Check: \((100-20)^2/8 = 800\). ✓ CORE (§7.6): units between \(Q_m\) and \(Q_c\) are valued by buyers above their marginal cost, but they are not produced. Those are mutually beneficial trades that do not happen. The outcome is Pareto inefficient.

Other costs of monopoly: X-inefficiency (without competitive pressure costs may drift above the minimum), rent-seeking (resources spent lobbying to win or protect monopoly), and possibly less innovation.

Can monopoly be efficient?

  • Natural monopoly: with large economies of scale, one firm has lower average cost than several. Splitting it would raise costs. Regulators then set price at AC (zero profit) or at MC with a subsidy, since MC < AC when AC is falling, so MC pricing makes a loss.
  • Innovation: patents give temporary monopoly to reward R&D (Schumpeter). The trade-off: higher prices now for more innovation later. Indian drug-pricing debates (Section 3(d) of the Patents Act, compulsory licensing) are about exactly this balance.
  • Policy responses (CORE §7.9): competition law (in India, the Competition Commission of India under the Competition Act 2002), regulation of utilities, public ownership (Goodwin Box 18.1 on privatising municipal water).

5.6 Price discrimination

Price discrimination: charging different prices for different units of the same good, for reasons not related to cost differences. It requires (1) market power; (2) the ability to identify or separate buyers with different willingness to pay or elasticities; and (3) no resale (arbitrage) between buyers.

First-degree (perfect) price discrimination

Each unit is sold at the buyer's maximum willingness to pay. The demand curve becomes the MR curve, since selling one more unit no longer lowers the price on earlier units. The firm produces until P = MC.

Key result

Perfect price discrimination eliminates the deadweight loss: the outcome is Pareto efficient. But all the surplus goes to the producer. This shows again that efficiency and fairness are different questions (Chapter 7).

Second-degree price discrimination

Price depends on the quantity bought or the version chosen, and buyers self-select: bulk discounts, two-part tariffs (fixed charge plus per-unit price), electricity tariff slabs, "economy vs premium" versions.

Third-degree price discrimination

Different prices for identifiable groups: student and senior-citizen concessions (Indian Railways), tourist vs local entry fees at monuments, different prices in different countries.

Derivation: third-degree price discrimination

Maximise \(\pi = TR_1(Q_1) + TR_2(Q_2) - C(Q_1 + Q_2)\).

FOCs: \(MR_1(Q_1) = MC\) and \(MR_2(Q_2) = MC\), so \(\boxed{MR_1 = MR_2 = MC}\). If MR differed across markets, moving a unit from the low-MR market to the high-MR market would raise revenue at the same cost.

Using \(MR_i = P_i(1 - 1/|\varepsilon_i|)\): \(P_1(1 - 1/|\varepsilon_1|) = P_2(1 - 1/|\varepsilon_2|)\). Hence \(P_1 > P_2\) ⇔ \(|\varepsilon_1| < |\varepsilon_2|\): the less elastic market pays the higher price.

Worked example

Market 1 (business travellers): \(P_1 = 100 - Q_1\). Market 2 (students): \(P_2 = 60 - Q_2\). MC = 20.

  • Market 1: \(100 - 2Q_1 = 20\) ⇒ \(Q_1 = 40\), \(P_1 = 60\), \(|\varepsilon_1| = 60/40 = 1.5\).
  • Market 2: \(60 - 2Q_2 = 20\) ⇒ \(Q_2 = 20\), \(P_2 = 40\), \(|\varepsilon_2| = 40/20 = 2\).
  • Profit = (60 − 20)(40) + (40 − 20)(20) = 1600 + 400 = 2000.
  • Single price: total demand \(Q = 160 - 2P\) (for P < 60) ⇒ \(P = 80 - Q/2\), MR = 80 − Q = 20 ⇒ Q = 60, P = 50. Profit = 30 × 60 = 1800 < 2000. Discrimination raises profit; here the students pay less and the business travellers more.

  • Lerner index \((P - MC)/P = 1/|\varepsilon|\). A monopolist always operates where demand is elastic.

Chapter 6Oligopoly and Game Theory

When a few firms dominate a market, each must anticipate its rivals. Game theory gives the tools (Nash equilibrium, best responses, backward induction), and the Cournot, Bertrand and Stackelberg duopoly models are the core applications.

Readings for this chapter
  • Oregon State Intermediate Microeconomics, Module 18, §18.1–18.3 (Cournot, Bertrand, Stackelberg), as assigned in Lecture 8.
  • CORE The Economy: South Asia, Unit 4: §4.1–4.3 (game theory, the invisible hand game, the prisoners' dilemma), §4.6 (repeated interaction).
  • Goodwin et al. Ch. 18, §3–4 (monopolistic competition and oligopoly).

6.1 What is oligopoly?

Key ideas
  • A limited number of sellers, each with some price-making ability.
  • Difficulty of entry (economies of scale, licences, brand loyalty, network effects).
  • Strategic interdependence: with few firms, each firm's best choice depends on what the others do. This leaves room for strategic behaviour.
  • Simplest case: duopoly (two sellers). Three classic models:
    1. Cournot: firms choose quantities simultaneously.
    2. Bertrand: firms choose prices simultaneously.
    3. Stackelberg: a leader chooses quantity first, then a follower.

Indian examples: telecom (Jio, Airtel, Vi), cement, aviation (IndiGo and Air India group), cola drinks, steel.

6.2 Game theory essentials

Game theory models strategic interaction as a game: players, the alternative actions (strategies) available to each, and the payoffs each receives for every combination of actions. A simultaneous two-player game is summarised in a payoff matrix (the first number is the row player's payoff).

Definitions
  • Best response: the action that gives a player the highest payoff, given the other player's action.
  • Dominant strategy: an action that is a best response to every action of the other player.
  • Pure-strategy Nash equilibrium (lecture definition): an action profile \((a_1^*, a_2^*)\) such that player 1 cannot obtain a higher payoff by choosing an action different from \(a_1^*\), given that player 2 adheres to \(a_2^*\), and vice versa. In other words, each player's action is a best response to the other's: no one wants to deviate on their own.

Example: a pricing game (prisoners' dilemma)

Two cement firms choose a High or Low price. Payoffs are profits in ₹ crore (Firm A, Firm B):

B: High priceB: Low price
A: High price10, 102, 15
A: Low price15, 25, 5
Solving for the Nash equilibrium (best-response method)

A's best responses: if B plays High, A compares 10 (High) with 15 (Low) ⇒ Low. If B plays Low, A compares 2 with 5 ⇒ Low. Low is a dominant strategy for A (underlined).

By symmetry Low is dominant for B.

The cell where both payoffs are best responses is (Low, Low) with payoffs (5, 5): the unique Nash equilibrium.

But (High, High) gives (10, 10), better for both. The equilibrium is Pareto inefficient for the firms. This is the prisoners' dilemma (CORE §4.3): individually rational choices lead to a collectively worse outcome.

Lessons: (1) collusion (cartels) is unstable, because each member has an incentive to cheat; (2) in repeated games, threats such as "I'll match your price cut forever" (tit-for-tat, grim trigger) can sustain cooperation (CORE §4.6); (3) for consumers the firms' dilemma is good news, since competition lowers prices. Explicit cartels are illegal under India's Competition Act 2002; the Competition Commission of India has fined cement makers for cartelisation.

6.3 The Cournot model (quantity competition)

Set-up (lecture): market demand \(P = A - BQ\) with \(Q = q_a + q_b\). Both firms have the same cost function \(C(q_i) = cq_i\) (constant MC = c). Each firm chooses its quantity to maximise profit, taking the other's output as given.

Derivation: reaction functions and the Cournot–Nash equilibrium

Firm a's profit: \(\pi_a = \big[A - B(q_a + q_b)\big]q_a - cq_a\).

FOC (treating \(q_b\) as fixed): \(\dfrac{\partial\pi_a}{\partial q_a} = A - 2Bq_a - Bq_b - c = 0\). (This is MRa = MC: firm a acts as a monopolist on the residual demand left by b.)

Solve for firm a's reaction (best-response) function: $$R_a:\quad q_a = \frac{A - c - Bq_b}{2B} = \frac{A-c}{2B} - \frac{q_b}{2}.$$ By symmetry: \(R_b:\ q_b = \dfrac{A - c - Bq_a}{2B}\). Each reaction function slopes down: if your rival produces more, you produce less.

Nash equilibrium: both are on their reaction functions at once. By symmetry \(q_a = q_b = q\): \(q = \dfrac{A - c - Bq}{2B}\) ⇒ \(2Bq + Bq = A - c\) ⇒ $$\boxed{q_a^* = q_b^* = \frac{A-c}{3B}},\qquad Q^* = \frac{2(A-c)}{3B}.$$

Price: \(P^* = A - B\cdot\dfrac{2(A-c)}{3B} = \boxed{\dfrac{A + 2c}{3}}\). Profit per firm: \(\pi^* = (P^* - c)q^* = \dfrac{A-c}{3}\cdot\dfrac{A-c}{3B} = \boxed{\dfrac{(A-c)^2}{9B}}\).

The Cournot equilibrium is a Nash equilibrium: at \((q_a^*, q_b^*)\) each firm's output is a best response to the other's, so neither wants to change.

Worked example: A = 100, B = 1, c = 10
  • Reaction functions: \(q_a = 45 - q_b/2\), \(q_b = 45 - q_a/2\).
  • Solve: \(q_a = 45 - (45 - q_a/2)/2 = 22.5 + q_a/4\) ⇒ \(\tfrac34 q_a = 22.5\) ⇒ \(q_a = 30\), so \(q_b = 30\).
  • Q = 60, P = 40, each profit = (40 − 10)(30) = 900, industry profit 1800.
  • Compare monopoly: \(Q = (A-c)/2B = 45\), P = 55, profit 2025. Competition: P = c = 10, Q = 90, zero profit.

Why collusion breaks down in Cournot

Collusion as a prisoners' dilemma

If the duopolists form a cartel and split monopoly output, each produces 22.5 and earns 2025/2 = 1012.5 (> 900). But if b keeps to 22.5, a's best response is \(q_a = 45 - 22.5/2 = 33.75\): P = 100 − 56.25 = 43.75 and \(\pi_a = 33.75^2 = 1139\). Payoffs for "Collude (22.5)" vs "Cournot (30)":

b: 22.5b: 30

Producing 30 is dominant, so the equilibrium is (30, 30), even though (22.5, 22.5) is better for both. Cartels like OPEC face exactly this cheating problem.

Extension: Cournot with n firms

Firm i: \(\pi_i = [A - B(q_i + Q_{-i})]q_i - cq_i\). FOC: \(A - 2Bq_i - BQ_{-i} - c = 0\). In a symmetric equilibrium \(Q_{-i} = (n-1)q\): \(A - c = B(n+1)q\) ⇒ $$q = \frac{A-c}{(n+1)B},\quad Q = \frac{n}{n+1}\cdot\frac{A-c}{B},\quad P = \frac{A + nc}{n+1}.$$ For n = 1 this is monopoly and for n = 2 Cournot duopoly. As \(n \to \infty\), \(P \to c\): the competitive outcome. More firms mean less market power.

6.4 The Bertrand model (price competition)

Set-up (lecture): two firms sell an identical product with the same constant marginal cost c and choose prices simultaneously. Consumers buy from the cheaper firm. Demand facing firm 1 is

$$q_1 = \begin{cases} a - bP_1 & \text{if } P_1 < P_2 \\[2pt] 0 & \text{if } P_1 > P_2 \\[2pt] \dfrac{a - bP}{2} & \text{if } P_1 = P_2 = P \end{cases}$$

and symmetrically for firm 2.

Derivation: the Bertrand equilibrium is \(P_1 = P_2 = c\)

Check every other possibility and show that someone wants to deviate:

\(P_1 > P_2 > c\): firm 1 sells nothing. It can undercut to just below \(P_2\), capture the whole market and earn a positive profit. Not an equilibrium.

\(P_1 = P_2 = P > c\): each gets half the market. By cutting price slightly to \(P - \epsilon\), a firm gets all the market at almost the same margin, nearly doubling its profit. Not an equilibrium.

\(P_1 > P_2 = c\): firm 2 makes zero profit and could raise its price a little (still below \(P_1\)) and earn a positive profit. Not an equilibrium.

Any \(P_i < c\): that firm makes losses on every unit sold and would rather raise its price. Not an equilibrium.

\(P_1 = P_2 = c\): each earns zero. Raising price loses all customers (still zero); cutting price means losses. No profitable deviation: this is the unique Nash equilibrium.

Quantities (the "??" on the slide): total demand at P = c is \(a - bc\), shared equally, so $$\boxed{q_1 = q_2 = \frac{a - bc}{2}},\qquad \pi_1 = \pi_2 = 0.$$ With \(Q = 100 - P\) (\(a = 100\), \(b = 1\)) and c = 10: P = 10 and each firm sells 45.

The Bertrand paradox

With just two firms the outcome is the same as perfect competition: P = MC and zero profit. Real duopolies usually earn profits, so the model's assumptions are relaxed: capacity constraints (a firm cannot serve the whole market, which brings back Cournot-like results), product differentiation (prices above MC), repeated interaction (tacit collusion), and cost differences (the low-cost firm prices just below the rival's MC).

6.5 The Stackelberg model (leader and follower)

Set-up: the same demand and costs as Cournot, but firm a (the leader, e.g. a dominant incumbent) chooses its quantity first. Firm b (the follower) observes \(q_a\) and then chooses \(q_b\). The decisions are sequential, reflecting the firms' different stature. The leader produces optimally knowing the follower will make the best of the resulting situation.

Derivation: solving by backward induction

Stage 2 (follower). Given \(q_a\), b maximises \([A - B(q_a + q_b)]q_b - cq_b\), which gives its Cournot reaction function: \(q_b(q_a) = \dfrac{A - c - Bq_a}{2B}\).

Stage 1 (leader). a knows b will respond this way, so it substitutes \(q_b(q_a)\) into its own profit: $$\pi_a = \Big[A - Bq_a - B\cdot\frac{A - c - Bq_a}{2B}\Big]q_a - cq_a = \Big[\frac{A - c}{2} - \frac{Bq_a}{2}\Big]q_a.$$

FOC: \(\dfrac{A-c}{2} - Bq_a = 0\) ⇒ \(\boxed{q_a^S = \dfrac{A-c}{2B}}\): the leader produces the monopoly output.

Follower: \(q_b^S = \dfrac{A - c - (A-c)/2}{2B} = \boxed{\dfrac{A-c}{4B}}\).

Total \(Q^S = \dfrac{3(A-c)}{4B}\), price \(P^S = A - BQ^S = \boxed{\dfrac{A + 3c}{4}}\).

Profits: \(\pi_a^S = \dfrac{A-c}{4}\cdot\dfrac{A-c}{2B} = \dfrac{(A-c)^2}{8B}\) and \(\pi_b^S = \dfrac{(A-c)^2}{16B}\).

Worked example: A = 100, B = 1, c = 10

Leader \(q_a = 45\); follower \(q_b = (90 - 45)/2 = 22.5\); Q = 67.5; P = 32.5. Profits: leader (32.5 − 10)(45) = 1012.5; follower 22.5 × 22.5 = 506.25.

First-mover advantage: the leader earns 1012.5 > 900 (Cournot) by committing to a large output, which forces the follower to contract. The follower is worse off (506.25 < 900). Consumers gain: Q is higher and P lower than under Cournot.

7.1 How markets order themselves

Class 1 opened with the campus mess-coupon market: no formal body governs buying and selling, yet coupons change hands at a fairly stable price. The mechanism is the feedback loop from Chapter 2: too high a price leaves unsold coupons, so sellers cut prices; too low a price creates a shortage, so buyers compete and prices rise. The price settles where demand equals supply, with no planner.

Adam Smith's invisible hand (assumes perfect competition)

In The Wealth of Nations (1776) Smith argued that individuals pursuing their own interest in competitive markets are "led by an invisible hand" to serve society. Bakers bake for their own gain, not out of kindness, yet we get bread. Prices signal what is scarce, and resources flow to where they are most wanted. Onion farmers want profit and buyers want a fair price; nobody plans for the city, yet Hyderabad has onions.

What it does not claim: that selfishness is always good. The outcome serves society only with competition and sound rules. The First Welfare Theorem makes this precise; Class 2 covers where it fails.

7.2 Consumer and producer surplus

  • Consumer surplus (CS): what buyers keep. It is the difference between the maximum each buyer is willing to pay (read off the demand curve) and the price actually paid, summed over all buyers. Tathya would pay up to ₹10 for onions; the price is ₹6; Tathya's surplus is ₹4.
  • Producer surplus (PS): what sellers keep. It is the price received minus the marginal cost of each unit (read off the supply curve), summed over all units. Jal can grow onions at ₹2 each and sells at ₹6, so Jal's surplus is ₹4.
  • Total surplus = CS + PS: the total gains from trade, the "size of the pie".
$$CS = \int_0^{Q^*}\big[P_d(q) - P^*\big]\,dq,\qquad PS = \int_0^{Q^*}\big[P^* - P_s(q)\big]\,dq.$$
Derivation: competitive equilibrium maximises total surplus

If quantity \(Q\) is traded, the total surplus is the area between demand (marginal benefit) and supply (marginal cost): \(TS(Q) = \int_0^{Q}\big[P_d(q) - P_s(q)\big]dq\). The price only splits this between buyers and sellers; it cancels in the total.

Maximise: \(\dfrac{dTS}{dQ} = P_d(Q) - P_s(Q) = 0\) ⇒ \(P_d(Q) = P_s(Q)\): exactly the competitive equilibrium quantity.

For \(Q < Q^*\), \(P_d > P_s\): some trades that would benefit both sides do not happen. For \(Q > Q^*\), \(P_d < P_s\): units cost more to make than they are worth to buyers.

So at equilibrium every trade where a buyer values the good more than it costs to produce actually happens (CORE §8.4). Anything that blocks trade shrinks the pie.

7.3 Taxes and deadweight loss

Derivation: the deadweight loss of a tax

With \(Q_d = a - bP_b\) and \(Q_s = c + dP_s\), a tax \(\tau\) drives a wedge \(P_b - P_s = \tau\). From Chapter 2, quantity falls by \(\Delta Q = \dfrac{bd}{b+d}\,\tau\).

The DWL is a triangle with height \(\tau\) and base \(\Delta Q\): $$DWL = \tfrac12\,\tau\,\Delta Q = \tfrac12\cdot\frac{bd}{b+d}\,\tau^2.$$

Course numbers: \(b = d = 1\), \(\tau = 2\): \(\Delta Q = 1\), DWL = 1. ✓

Two lessons: (i) DWL rises with the square of the tax, so doubling the tax quadruples the DWL; (ii) DWL is larger when demand and supply are more elastic (larger b, d). Taxing inelastic goods distorts less.

Onion vs alcohol (class question). For onions the tax is pure inefficiency: lost trades that benefited both sides. For alcohol, the "lost trades" may include consumption that harms others (drunk driving, health costs borne by the public). Then the market quantity was too high to begin with and the tax can raise welfare. That is a Pigouvian tax, covered under externalities below.

7.4 Price controls

Price ceiling (cap)

Derivation: welfare under the cap

At P = 4: \(Q_s = 4 - 2 = 2\) and \(Q_d = 10 - 4 = 6\). Trade is limited by the short side: Q = 2.

PS = \(\tfrac12(4 - 2)(2) = 2\).

CS (best case, units go to those who value them most) = area between demand and 4 for Q from 0 to 2 = \(\tfrac{(10-4) + (8-4)}{2}\times 2 = 10\).

Total = 12, so DWL = 16 − 12 = 4: the triangle between demand and supply from Q = 2 to 4, \(\tfrac12(8-4)(4-2) = 4\).

Who gets the 2 onions? With a shortage the price no longer rations, so something else does: queues, contacts, black markets, or a ration card system. If the good goes to people with lower willingness to pay, CS is even lower and the DWL larger. The class point: the cap aims to help buyers, but it can make them worse off overall.

Price floor

A price floor above equilibrium (minimum wage, minimum support prices) creates a surplus: \(Q_s > Q_d\).

India focus: minimum support prices (MSP)

Suppose the government guarantees P = 8 in our market. Then \(Q_d = 2\) and \(Q_s = 6\): a surplus of 4 units. If the government does not buy the surplus, only 2 units trade: CS = ½(10−8)(2) = 2, PS = ((8−2)+(8−4))/2 × 2 = 10, total = 12, DWL = 4. In India the Food Corporation of India procures much of the surplus wheat and rice at MSP, so farmers sell all 6 units. This supports farm incomes, but it costs the government 8 × 4 = 32 and builds large grain stocks, and it encourages over-production of the supported crops (wheat and paddy in Punjab and Haryana, with groundwater depletion: a commons problem, see the commons section). Goodwin Box 6.1 discusses the parallel debate over minimum wages.

7.5 Pareto efficiency

Definitions (CORE §5.2)
  • An allocation is Pareto efficient if no one can be made better off without making someone else worse off.
  • A Pareto improvement makes at least one person better off and nobody worse off.
  • The set of Pareto-efficient allocations forms the Pareto efficiency curve (frontier).

Coupon example: the seller values a coupon at ₹20 and the buyer at ₹40. Before trade both could gain, so the situation is not efficient. After a trade at ₹30 (seller gains 10, buyer gains 10) no free gain remains. Any price between 20 and 40 would be efficient; the price only decides the split.

Efficient is not the same as fair

Efficiency says nothing about who gets what. Choosing among points on the frontier is a question of equity, which markets do not settle; society has to (CORE §5.3). This is the same idea as a Pareto front in multi-objective optimisation.

7.6 The welfare theorems

First Fundamental Theorem of Welfare Economics

Under these assumptions, every competitive equilibrium is Pareto efficient:

  1. Perfect competition: many buyers and sellers; no one can set the price. (If this fails: market power.)
  2. No externalities: every cost and benefit falls on the people trading. (If this fails: externalities.)
  3. Full information: both sides know the price and the quality of what they trade. (If this fails: information asymmetry, e.g. used cars and "lemons", CORE §12.6.)
  4. Private goods with clear property rights: goods are rival, excludable and can be owned and traded. (If this fails: public goods and commons.)

The mess-coupon market satisfies all four: many students on WhatsApp, no spillovers, everyone knows what a coupon is. Every trade that helps both sides happens.

Second Fundamental Theorem

Under similar (plus convexity) assumptions, any Pareto-efficient allocation can be achieved as a competitive equilibrium after a suitable lump-sum redistribution of initial endowments. In short: redistribute first, then let markets work. The theorems separate efficiency (left to markets) from equity (a social choice). In practice lump-sum transfers are hard to design: real taxes create DWLs, so there is often an efficiency–equity trade-off.

7.7 Market failure: externalities

An externality is a cost or benefit that falls on people who are not part of the transaction. Decision-makers count only their own costs and benefits, so the market makes too much of what harms others and too little of what helps them.

Derivation: the externality wedge

Private producers supply where price = marginal private cost (MPC). Society's cost includes the marginal external cost (MEC): \(MSC = MPC + MEC\).

Worked example: the Coase theorem

A factory's dumping causes fishermen ₹70,000 of damage a year. A filter that stops it costs the factory ₹40,000. Efficient outcome: install the filter (40 < 70).

  • Fishermen own the right to a clean lake: the factory must either pay ₹70,000 in compensation or spend ₹40,000 on the filter. It installs the filter.
  • The factory has the right to pollute: the fishermen are willing to pay up to ₹70,000 for clean water, and the factory accepts anything above ₹40,000. They agree on a payment between 40 and 70 thousand and the filter is installed.

Coase theorem: if property rights are well defined and bargaining costs are low, private bargaining reaches the efficient outcome whatever the initial allocation of rights. The allocation of rights affects only the distribution (who pays). It fails with many affected parties (thousands of residents near a plant), high transaction costs, unclear rights or asymmetric information, and the poor may be unable to "pay" for clean air (CORE §12.2–12.3).

Derivation: cap and trade minimises abatement cost

Two firms must together cut emissions by 40 tonnes. Marginal abatement costs: \(MAC_1 = A_1\), \(MAC_2 = 3A_2\).

Total cost \(TC = \int_0^{A_1} a\,da + \int_0^{A_2} 3a\,da = \tfrac12A_1^2 + \tfrac32A_2^2\), subject to \(A_1 + A_2 = 40\).

Minimising: \(\dfrac{d}{dA_1}\big[\tfrac12A_1^2 + \tfrac32(40 - A_1)^2\big] = A_1 - 3(40 - A_1) = 0\) ⇒ \(\boxed{MAC_1 = MAC_2}\) ⇒ \(A_1 = 30\), \(A_2 = 10\).

Cost = ½(900) + 1.5(100) = 450 + 150 = 600. A uniform rule (20 each) costs ½(400) + 1.5(400) = 200 + 600 = 800.

With tradable permits, each firm abates until its MAC equals the permit price. The market price settles at 30 where the cap is met. The low-cost firm does more of the cutting and sells permits to the high-cost firm. A carbon tax of 30 per tonne would give the same allocation.

Public goods and free-riding

Derivation: the efficient quantity of a public good (Samuelson condition)

Because the good is non-rival, every unit benefits all users at once. The social marginal benefit is the vertical sum of individual marginal benefits: \(MSB(Q) = \sum_i MB_i(Q)\). (For private goods we sum demand curves horizontally.)

Efficiency: \(\boxed{\sum_i MB_i = MC}\).

Example: three residents value street lights at \(MB_1 = 60 - Q\), \(MB_2 = 40 - Q\), \(MB_3 = 20 - Q\); MC = 75 per light. \(\sum MB = 120 - 3Q = 75\) ⇒ \(Q^* = 15\).

Private provision: even resident 1, who values lights most, would buy only where \(60 - Q = 75\), i.e. none. And because the good is non-excludable, each resident hopes the others will pay and free-rides. Result: under-provision, often zero.

Solutions: tax-financed public provision; community norms with peer punishment of free-riders (CORE §4.7 shows experimentally that punishment sustains contributions); making the good excludable (turning it into a club good).

The tragedy of the commons

A shared lake with one fish stock: each user keeps the whole gain from fishing more but shares the cost (fewer fish for everyone), so each over-uses and everyone ends worse off. It is a negative externality with no owner to charge for it, and a many-player prisoners' dilemma.

Derivation: open access vs the efficient number of boats

With \(N\) boats, total catch value is \(N\cdot AP(N)\) with \(AP = 100 - N\) (₹ thousand per boat, falling because the boats share a fixed stock). Each boat costs 20.

Open access: a new boat enters as long as its own catch covers its cost: \(AP(N) \ge 20\) ⇒ entry until \(100 - N = 20\) ⇒ \(N_{open} = 80\). Total rent = \(80(20 - 20) = 0\): the resource's value is completely dissipated.

Efficient: maximise total rent \(R = N(100 - N) - 20N\). \(dR/dN = 100 - 2N - 20 = 0\) ⇒ \(N^* = 40\). Equivalently set the marginal product \(MP = 100 - 2N\) equal to cost. Rent = \(40(60 - 20) = 1600\).

Why the gap: the entrant compares its average catch with cost and ignores that it lowers every other boat's catch by \(|dAP/dN| = 1\) each. That is an external cost of \(N\) per entrant. Open access gives twice the efficient effort here.

Why climate change is harder

  • No world government: no one can enforce rules across countries, so agreements rely on voluntary compliance.
  • Free-riding by countries: every country gains from others' cuts, so each is tempted to wait (CORE §4.14).
  • Costs now, benefits later: cuts pay off decades away, mostly for people not yet born.
  • Unequal responsibility: countries differ in who caused the problem, who is hurt most and who can afford to act. This is India's "common but differentiated responsibilities" position.
  • Uncertainty and tipping points: damage is hard to measure, and some changes may be irreversible.

State the First Welfare Theorem and give one real example for the failure of each assumption.

Competitive equilibria are Pareto efficient if there is (1) perfect competition (failure: a pharmaceutical patent monopoly), (2) no externalities (failure: stubble burning and Delhi's air), (3) full information (failure: used-car "lemons", health insurance), (4) private goods with clear property rights (failure: groundwater over-extraction, street lighting).

Explain why the Coase theorem says the allocation of property rights does not matter for efficiency. Why might it still matter in practice?

With costless bargaining the parties will always strike the deal that maximises joint surplus, and the rights only decide who pays whom. In practice transaction costs, many affected parties, unclear rights, information problems and wealth constraints (the poor cannot pay polluters) prevent efficient bargains, and the distribution of rights is itself a fairness question.

Exit question from Class 1: give an example where a market outcome is efficient but you would call it unfair.

Sample answer: during a flood, bottled water sells at a very high competitive price; every trade is voluntary and the allocation may be efficient, but poorer families go without. Or: perfect price discrimination is efficient but leaves consumers no surplus. Efficiency concerns the size of the pie, fairness its division.
